# James Joyce and Nora Barnacle

## Short version

James Joyce saw Nora Barnacle on Nassau Street in Dublin on June 10, 1904. Nora was 20, newly arrived from Galway, and working at Finn's Hotel as a chambermaid/waitress. Joyce approached her and asked to see her again. One account allows that a mutual acquaintance may instead have introduced them, so the exact mechanics of the first words are not completely certain.

They arranged a meeting for June 14; Nora did not show. Joyce sent a note to Finn's Hotel asking for another chance. They met on June 16 and walked toward Ringsend. That date became the calendar date of *Ulysses* and later Bloomsday.

Within four months they left Ireland together for continental Europe. They remained partners for the rest of Joyce's life, had two children, and did not formally marry until 1931.

## The first encounter

- **Date:** June 10, 1904.
- **Place:** Nassau Street, Dublin, near Finn's Hotel.
- **Nora:** 20, from Galway, working at Finn's Hotel after leaving home.
- **Joyce:** 22, a recently graduated would-be writer and singer, living an unsettled Dublin life.

The Dictionary of Irish Biography says Joyce was strongly attracted to Nora while she was walking on Nassau Street and probably approached her; it notes that an introduction through Vincent Cosgrave is another possibility.

The Morgan Library's Joyce exhibition gives the sequence cleanly: they spoke, arranged another meeting, Nora failed to appear, Joyce left a note at Finn's Hotel, and they finally met on June 16.

## June 16

They walked to Ringsend. Later letters and Joyce scholarship indicate that the outing became sexually intimate. Biographers have often understood later references by Joyce to Nora having "made" him a man as referring to manual sexual contact on that walk. The precise sexual reconstruction is based on later testimony and letters rather than a contemporaneous diary, so it should be labeled as biographical inference rather than a camera-record fact.

Whatever the exact details, June 16 clearly mattered enormously to Joyce. He eventually set all of *Ulysses* on that date.

## What made it continue

This was not a match built around literary similarity. Nora did not share Joyce's education or literary ambitions. What he seems to have valued was her directness, humor, sensuality, independence, and Galway speech. Her manner and language fed into his fiction, especially aspects of Molly Bloom and Gretta Conroy.

The courtship also had a fast practical escalation. By autumn 1904 Nora agreed to leave Ireland with him. On October 8 they departed together, beginning the long expatriate life that took them through Trieste, Zurich, and Paris.

Their surviving letters, especially during separations, show that sexual attraction remained an important part of the bond, alongside jealousy, dependence, family life, and Joyce's reliance on Nora as the stable person in an otherwise unstable career.

## Why this case is useful for Shallow

This one is almost the opposite of an app-mediated match:

1. a person noticed another person moving through ordinary public space;
2. he initiated;
3. the first arranged meeting failed;
4. he followed up rather than interpreting the miss as the end;
5. the successful date became intensely memorable;
6. they made a major life decision together only months later.

It is also a useful warning against matching only on stated interests. On paper Joyce and Nora were poorly matched in education and literary taste. Their actual compatibility ran through temperament, erotic attraction, humor, practical loyalty, and the way each affected the other's sense of self.
