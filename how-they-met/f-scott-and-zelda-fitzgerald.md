# F. Scott Fitzgerald and Zelda Sayre Fitzgerald

## Short version

They met at a country-club dance in Montgomery, Alabama, in July 1918. Fitzgerald was a 21-year-old Army second lieutenant stationed at nearby Camp Sheridan. Zelda Sayre had just finished high school and was already a conspicuous figure in Montgomery's social life. Contemporary chronology and later biographies agree that Scott noticed her at the dance and pursued her quickly.

The relationship then became a long-distance courtship built heavily out of letters, visits, symbolic gifts, jealousy, and a recurring practical question: could Scott become successful enough to support the life Zelda expected? They became engaged, broke the engagement in 1919 when his prospects looked poor, resumed after Scribner's accepted *This Side of Paradise*, and married in New York on April 3, 1920, just after the novel appeared.

## The first meeting

- **Place:** a dance at the Montgomery Country Club.
- **Date:** July 1918.
- **Scott:** 21, an Army officer at Camp Sheridan, still trying to become a published novelist.
- **Zelda:** 18, recently graduated from Sidney Lanier High School, already well known in Montgomery's dance and country-club world.
- One biography describes Zelda opening a country-club dance with a solo; Fitzgerald asked people around him who she was and then worked his way onto the dance floor.
- By September 1918 Fitzgerald's private ledger recorded that he had fallen in love.

This was not a slow friendship that unexpectedly became romantic. The record looks much more like immediate attention followed by persistent pursuit.

## What happened next

Very early, Scott folded Zelda into his writing. In August 1918 he sent her a chapter of his unpublished novel *The Romantic Egotist* and told her its heroine resembled her. Zelda gave him an engraved flask in September. Their courtship was already mixing flirtation, self-dramatization, literature, gifts, and the idea that each was becoming material in the other's life.

When military service and then Scott's move to New York separated them, letters became central. Surviving correspondence shows an intensely romantic style on both sides. In March 1919 Scott sent Zelda an engagement ring. The engagement was unstable, however, because Scott had little money and no secure career.

By June 1919 Zelda broke the engagement. Scott's literary and financial failure were not side issues: in their own later accounts, love and money became tightly linked. Scott quit his advertising job, returned to St. Paul, and rewrote his novel. Scribner's accepted *This Side of Paradise* on September 16, 1919. He immediately renewed contact with Zelda, their engagement resumed, and they married the following April.

## Why this case is useful for Shallow

The story is unusually clear about the difference between **attraction** and **relationship viability**.

The attraction appears to have been fast: a crowded social setting, visual presence, dancing, social reputation, and Scott's immediate pursuit. The relationship persisted through a different mechanism: letters, repeated visits, literary self-disclosure, and symbolic gestures. The major obstacle was not lack of feeling but Zelda's doubt that Scott could build a viable life with her.

The career event that changed the relationship was external and concrete: publication. That did not create the attraction, but it changed the answer to "can this actually work?"
