# Sources and access

Priority: studies and survey files already designed for statistical analysis. Court documents may contain vivid language but are selective, contested and unusually revealing about private lives. They should **not** be a general-purpose corpus of individual divorces.

## National surveys and longitudinal data

**CDC/NCHS — National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG).** Marriage, cohabitation, divorce, household and family-formation survey data. Public-use files are available; the CDC distinguishes restricted-use geographic data and research-data-center access. Good for rates, relationship histories and differences across broad groups. Not a source of paired courtroom dialogue. Its samples and age coverage changed over time, so specify release/cohort.
- https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nsfg/index.htm
- https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nsfg/about_nsfg.htm
- https://cdc.gov/rdc/restricted-nchs-variables/nsfg.html

**U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — NLSY79.** A longitudinal study connecting respondents' work histories and family transitions across decades. A 2024 published analysis studies marriage and divorce through age 55 in the cohort born 1957–1964. Useful for the effect *timing* of economic and work changes on descriptive outcomes; not permission to infer causal mechanisms without appropriate methods.
- https://www.bls.gov/nls/nlsy79.htm
- https://www.bls.gov/mlr/2024/article/patterns-of-marriage-and-divorce-from-ages-15-to-55-evidence-from-the-nlsy79.htm

**U.S. Census Bureau — Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP).** A longitudinal survey recording changing employment, earnings, household composition and family circumstances over time. Potential source for temporal association between labor-market shocks and household changes. Inspect exact variable availability and panel comparability before making claims.
- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/sipp.html

**CDC/NCHS — marriage and divorce rates.** Useful for the national/statistical denominator, *not* individual reasons. NCHS suspended routine collection of detailed divorce records beginning in 1996; modern headline rates do not supply the claimed motives or individual life histories.
- https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/marriage-divorce.htm

## How people explain divorce and staying together

**Paul R. Amato and Denise Previti (2003), “People's Reasons for Divorcing: Gender, Social Class, the Life Course, and Adjustment.”** *Journal of Family Issues* 24(5), 602–626. DOI: 10.1177/0192513X03254507. Analyzed open-ended divorce reasons from 208 respondents to national panel surveys collected 1980–1997. Infidelity, incompatibility, substance use and growing apart featured in the responses. The authors found more blame placed on former spouses than on the speakers themselves. Treat as coded *accounts*, not validated causal explanations.
- https://doi.org/10.1177/0192513X03254507

**Shelby B. Scott, Galena K. Rhoades, Scott M. Stanley, Elizabeth S. Allen and Howard J. Markman (2013), “Reasons for Divorce and Recollections of Premarital Intervention: Implications for Improving Relationship Education.”** *Couple and Family Psychology: Research and Practice* 2(2), 131–145. DOI: 10.1037/a0032025. Interviews with 52 people who had received a particular premarital education program; 18 couples had both former spouses interviewed. Reported leading contributors include lack of commitment, infidelity and conflict; reported “final straw” reasons also include violence and substance abuse. This is a highly selected, modest sample and is **not** a population divorce-rate survey. Researchers report relevant financial interests involving the intervention.
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24818068/
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4012696/

**Denise Previti and Paul R. Amato (2003), “Why Stay Married? Rewards, Barriers, and Marital Stability.”** *Journal of Marriage and Family* 65(3), 561–573. DOI: 10.1111/j.1741-3737.2003.00561.x. Studies people's stated reasons to stay married and relates them to later divorce. Essential comparison for Shallow: do not study only the people who split up.
- https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2003.00561.x

## Court records and publication constraints

**Michigan MiCOURT Case Search:** its terms say data are for informational use, not a substitute for official court files, and **bulk downloads are prohibited**. Do not scrape it or republish a divorce docket corpus. State- and court-specific rules also govern access to sensitive family filings.
- https://micourt.courts.michigan.gov/case-search

The most useful part of a published judicial decision for Shallow may be already **summarized recurring categories**, with selection bias made explicit. Prefer sourcing published aggregate studies to harvesting people's allegations. A court's finding and an advocate's allegation are different kinds of evidence.

## Statistical privacy

**NIST SP 800-188 (2023), *De-Identifying Government Datasets: Techniques and Governance*.** Removing a name is not enough; evaluate linkage, access controls and reidentification risks, particularly when many variables can be combined.
- https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/188/final

**U.S. Census Bureau, “Statistical Safeguards” and “About Data Privacy on Business & the Economy.”** Aggregate publication may still reveal individuals through sparse cells or reconstructible tables. Coarsening, cell suppression, complementary suppression and, where appropriate, noise infusion are relevant techniques.
- https://www.census.gov/about/policies/privacy/statistical_safeguards.html
- https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/disclosure/about.html

## Source admission checklist

For every proposed analysis note: identify population, interview date, relationship duration measure, case selection, interview timing, number of unique people/couples, the observable behavior versus the speaker's interpretation, comparison group, uncertainty, source terms, and whether aggregation meets privacy requirements. No person-level material goes into this public repository.
