# Initial findings — research notes, not Shallow's own dataset

These findings come from published studies. We have **not** extracted, stored, or coded individual divorce records, and have not produced any independent estimates yet. Do not present any of the percentages below as the likelihood of divorce for a person marrying in 2026.

## Three useful statistical distinctions

1. **Marriage histories are cohort-dependent.** In a 2024 BLS analysis of NLSY79 participants born in 1957–1964, by age 55 approximately 87% had ever married, 40% had ever divorced and 46% **of those who had married** had ever divorced. The study selected 5,766 respondents for its analysis of 6,658 marriages. “46% of ever-married people in this cohort” is not interchangeable with “46% of today's marriages will end in divorce.” [BLS analysis](https://www.bls.gov/mlr/2024/article/patterns-of-marriage-and-divorce-from-ages-15-to-55-evidence-from-the-nlsy79.htm)

2. **Self-reported causes are an object of study.** Amato and Previti (2003) coded open-ended accounts from 208 people. Their abstract identifies infidelity, incompatibility, substance use and growing apart among common explanations, and finds that former spouses were more likely to attribute blame to the ex-partner than to themselves. That is evidence about *how divorced people explain their divorce*, not adjudication of who caused it. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0192513X03254507)

3. **Paired accounts can diverge.** Scott and colleagues (2013) interviewed 52 divorced participants in a selected premarital-education sample, including 18 couples in which both former partners were interviewed. In that sample the reported contributors most often mentioned included lack of commitment, infidelity and arguments. Interviewing both parties allows measurement of agreement rather than treating either single account as the relationship's complete history. This sample should not be used for population prediction, and the authors disclosed financial interests in the educational intervention. [Paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24818068/)

A complementary research question is **why people stay**: Previti and Amato (2003) studied reasons to stay married and their relationship to divorce over subsequent years. Any risk-warning system needs people whose marriages *continue* in its comparison group, rather than learning exclusively from marriages selected because they ended. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2003.00561.x)

## Agenda for aggregate analyses

- **Time:** married for less than 5, 5–9, 10–19, 20+ years; follow-up period stated.
- **Economic shocks:** temporary unemployment vs long job loss; income *bands* and broad shifts; paid work as well as unpaid work; understand reverse causation.
- **Housing:** counts of moves and broad tenure categories instead of individual ZIP trails.
- **Care:** childbirth, dependents, eldercare and illness, subject to the actual variables each dataset makes available.
- **Speech:** how often explanations assign responsibility to self, partner, both or circumstance; how often complaints are about workload, trust, promises, sex, finances, affection, intimidation or failed repair.
- **Role blindness:** classify without assuming which party is husband or wife. Add sex/gender strata only if the research question, study design and privacy permit.
- **Safety:** quantify harmful conduct or credible allegations where evidence permits; never misclassify threats and coercion as routine “give and take.”
- **Positive counterfactual:** which hardships are also frequent among relationships that endure, and what evidence—if any—supports specific responses as helpful?

Do not treat a common *reason given for divorce* as proof that the same factor *predicts* divorce. For that question, use prospective data, an at-risk denominator and a stated observation window.

## Example language for Shallow (invented, not quoted from anyone)

These are **synthetic questions** to illustrate applications, not statistical findings:

> “If your partner loses work for a year, what are you willing to change about your own life?”

> “Suppose both of you sincerely believe you have done more than half the household work. How do you settle the disagreement without building a ledger of grievances?”

> “What promise are you prepared to keep when you are disappointed, tired, or no longer getting the response you hoped for?”

> “What should you never be expected to tolerate, regardless of the length of the marriage?”

The warning is not that happiness is impossible or that distress is always inevitable. It is that marriage is a commitment made under uncertainty about work, health, family, desire and money. Shallow should make the uncertainty and the obligations explicit before people commit, while preserving their right to leave a damaging or unsafe relationship.
