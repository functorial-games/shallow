# Why people divorce

Research notes for Shallow. The central subject is **how spouses describe each other and the relationship as it deteriorates**, not a list of legal grounds for dissolution.

Shallow is intended to support durable, healthy relationships, including established marriages. Studying unsuccessful marriages should sharpen questions about commitment, generosity, burdens, honesty, resentment, changing expectations, and the work people are agreeing to undertake. It must not turn into pressure to remain with someone violent or coercive.

## What to collect

Prefer first-person testimony, interviews, statements, and contemporary accounts over judicial paraphrase. Preserve the difference between what a person **said**, what they **believed**, what they **admitted**, what another party **disputed**, and what a court or other reliable source **established**.

For each union, where the source supports it:

- Relationship duration (dating, cohabiting, married, separated); annotate events by both calendar time and time since the relationship began.
- Each partner's words about the other and about their own obligations, disappointments, choices, and contributions.
- **Contrasting accounts:** Party A's description of an event beside Party B's, without automatically deciding which account is correct.
- Household trajectory: number of residences, moves, and approximate regions; children/dependents as *coarsely described* context; changes in caregiving and work.
- Finances over time: each party's employment and income band, household income band, debt, housing and asset changes, job loss or major work-hour changes.
- Events: illness, pregnancy, childrearing, prolonged separation, caretaking, infidelity, addiction, external family pressure, financial shocks, threats, violence, attempts to repair, reconciliations.
- What each partner says they did not wish to do but did, refused to do, expected the other to do, or resented doing.
- Outcome and source limitations: settled/unsettled, separated/divorced/reconciled; who supplied evidence and who did not.

Explicitly code speech acts: accusation, complaint, demand, concession, invitation to repair, apology, threat, boundary, account of a sacrifice, revision of an earlier account. Keep characteristic words such as “always,” “never,” “owe,” “fair,” and “I tried” when lawful to quote; distinguish direct quotation from editorial summary.

## Two views of the same case

1. **Role-blind reading (default):** Party A and Party B, with gender, race, names, occupation, and other unnecessary identity markers suppressed. Ask what the words alone imply.
2. **Role-aware analysis (optional):** Retain sex/gender or another contextual characteristic only where genuinely documented, permissible to use, and relevant to a stated research question. Examine differences without treating stereotypes as findings or projecting them onto individuals.

Gender removal should be reversible **in research metadata only when legitimately available**, never by modifying or falsifying the original record. A reader should be able to tell exactly which elements were suppressed.

## Design principles

- A court filing is not a finding of fact. An interview reports a person's perspective. Agreement between spouses does not necessarily establish truth; disagreement does not necessarily imply that both contributed equally.
- Threats, violence, intimidation and coercive control require their own event and safety flags. “Party A threatened Party B with a knife” is not merely an example of mutual communication trouble. Do not give symmetrical responsibility for one-sided harm.
- Do not use longevity as the sole success measure. A marriage can last a long time while being unsafe or miserable. Track continuation, quality and safety separately when evidence exists.
- Never impute values for missing dates, incomes, residences, gender or claimed events. “Not stated,” “not available,” and “no” are distinct.
- Quote words in their setting (a complaint, sworn evidence, an interview, a recollection years later), because they are different kinds of speech.
- Keep sampling denominators visible. Published judgments overrepresent high-conflict, financially complex or appealed disputes and are **not** a representative sample of divorce; interview studies have their own sampling biases.
- Work backward from a divorce **and** forward from relationships that did not end; otherwise one cannot distinguish correlates of divorce from common marital difficulties.

## Privacy and publication

This is a **public GitHub repository**. Do **not** commit raw divorce pleadings, verbatim sensitive allegations tied to identifying details, children's information, exact residential addresses or ZIP histories, raw earnings, phone numbers, case numbers, or information obtained contrary to access terms. Publicly available does not mean appropriate for redistribution into a searchable AI corpus.

Research working copies, if later authorized, need restricted storage, retention periods, and a reviewed release policy. A public example must be synthetic or transformed sufficiently to prevent re-identification. Coarse regions, income *bands*, approximate date intervals, and generic event descriptions are preferable for public notes; even combinations of these can identify families. Linking publicly to lawfully published judgments is different from storing their contents.

## Questions for Shallow's matchmaker

- When you say “I do all the work,” what work are you counting? What would the other person count?
- What have you continued to do for your partner after you stopped wanting to do it?
- What changed after a child, move, job loss, shift change or illness?
- What do you think your partner would say happened in the same argument?
- What is a sacrifice you would make, and what is a boundary you would not cross?
- What kinds of disagreement can be borne generously? What behavior would make staying unsafe?

These questions should illuminate obligations without disclosing private relationship confidences to outsiders, and without turning the matchmaker into a judge of either spouse.
