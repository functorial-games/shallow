# Comparative history of marriage and divorce

This folder preserves and rebuilds the earlier Shallow research thread on marriage across legal systems, religions, classes, and continents.

The object is not a single timeless institution. “Marriage” can combine or separate:

- a sexual union;
- co-residence and household production;
- a public or ritual status;
- an alliance between kin groups;
- transfer of dowry, bridewealth, land, labor, or inheritance rights;
- recognized parentage and legitimacy;
- rights over children;
- mutual support and care;
- exclusive sexual claims;
- authority over a spouse;
- immigration, citizenship, rank, caste, or freedom status;
- a moral, religious, or sacramental bond;
- a legal procedure for dissolution and settlement.

Comparisons must ask which of these functions a given society bundled together, who could obtain the status, and what happened when people lived together without it.

## Central questions

1. **Formation:** What acts created the union—consent, sex, transfer of property, kin approval, contract, registration, public ceremony, religious rite, or cohabitation?
2. **Dissolution:** Could either spouse leave? By unilateral repudiation, mutual consent, judicial order, kin action, abandonment, sale, or simply ending co-residence?
3. **Remarriage:** Could divorced and widowed people remarry, and under what waiting periods or property constraints?
4. **Plural unions:** Could one person have more than one recognized spouse, concubine, secondary partner, or household?
5. **Property:** Who owned dowry, bridewealth, wages, land, tools, household goods, and inheritances during and after marriage?
6. **Children:** How were parentage, legitimacy, custody, guardianship, residence, support, inheritance, and freedom status assigned?
7. **Work and care:** Which spouse's labor sustained the household, and was unpaid care legally or economically recognized?
8. **Class and status:** Did the same rules apply to elites, peasants, artisans, wage laborers, enslaved people, servants, soldiers, migrants, and the propertyless?
9. **Doctrine and practice:** What did jurists, clergy, or rulers prescribe, and what do contracts, court cases, letters, chronicles, parish records, ethnography, and archaeology show people actually did?
10. **State and religious power:** When and why did households become more legible to churches, courts, landlords, tax systems, colonial states, and modern bureaucracies?

## Provisional theses to test

### There is no universal historical package called marriage

Societies regularly separate sex, household membership, property partnership, parentage, and public status. Translating every recognized union as “marriage” can conceal important differences; refusing the word whenever a union differs from modern Western civil marriage can conceal equally important continuities.

### Lifelong indissolubility is historically particular

Classical Roman marriage was ordinarily dissoluble. Early Irish law recognized several forms of union and grounds for separation. Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Jewish, Islamic, Chinese, and many other legal traditions provided forms of divorce, repudiation, mutual dissolution, or remarriage.

The mature Latin-Christian doctrine that a valid consummated marriage formed an indissoluble sacramental bond was not the universal ancient starting point. It developed through late-antique theology, ecclesiastical legislation, papal decisions, canon-law synthesis, church jurisdiction, and later reforms. The high-medieval canonists gave it a systematic legal form; the Council of Trent's decree *Tametsi* in 1563 imposed a public canonical form in the regions where it was promulgated while reaffirming sacramental permanence.

This claim needs precision. “The Church invented indissoluble marriage” would be too crude: Jewish and Christian texts already contained strong restrictions; late Roman emperors restricted divorce; Eastern Christian traditions developed differently; actual practice often resisted doctrine; annulment and separation remained available; Protestant reformers later permitted divorce and remarriage on specified grounds.

### Legal marriage often organizes property and parentage before it expresses private feeling

Dowry, bridewealth, household succession, legitimacy, inheritance, alliance, citizenship, and control of labor recur across very different systems. This does not mean affection was absent. It means legal records preserve the consequences institutions cared to enforce.

### Propertyless people expose the limits of the category

When neither partner owns land, transmits a dowry, controls a workshop, or leaves an estate, some elite functions of marriage disappear. But formal status can still determine:

- whether cohabitation is permitted;
- whether children are free, legitimate, supported, or inheriting;
- whether a partner may be sold, transferred, expelled, or separated;
- whether a widow or abandoned partner has maintenance rights;
- whether the union is publicly acknowledged;
- which kin group receives the children;
- whether either person can legally remarry;
- whether church or state courts will hear a claim.

Roman *contubernium* is a stark case: enslaved partners could form durable unions, but Roman law did not treat them as legal marriage; children followed the mother's enslaved status, and the relationship furnished weak protection against separation. Medieval canon law gradually asserted the validity of marriages of the unfree, yet masters and economic dependency still constrained actual cohabitation and family integrity.

The research question is therefore not simply “Did marriage matter without property?” It is:

> Which consequences of marital status remained material when a couple had little property, and which parts of elite marriage became irrelevant or inaccessible?

### Sources systematically overrepresent property

Marriage charters, dowry contracts, inheritance disputes, and elite chronicles survive because property and rank generated writing. Informal unions among poor people may appear only when something went wrong: prosecution, bastardy proceedings, parish relief, manorial fines, domestic violence, abandonment, enslavement, or a dispute over children.

Absence of a contract is not evidence that poor people had no lasting union. Presence of a legal ideal is not evidence that ordinary people followed it.

## Geographic and chronological modules

The initial archive should keep these modules distinct before attempting broad synthesis:

1. Mesopotamia: Sumerian and Old Babylonian contracts and law collections, especially Hammurabi.
2. Egypt: Pharaonic, Ptolemaic, Roman, Coptic, Islamic, Ottoman, and modern periods.
3. Greece: Athens, Gortyn, Hellenistic variation, and household/citizenship rules.
4. Rome: republican and classical marriage, concubinage, *contubernium*, late-antique Christian legislation, Justinian.
5. Jewish law and practice: biblical texts, Second Temple Judea, rabbinic law, ketubah, get, medieval communities, modern denominational and state forms.
6. Latin Christianity: patristic thought, councils, Gratian, papal decretals, Lateran IV, ecclesiastical courts, Trent, regional practice.
7. Eastern Christianity: Byzantine civil and canon law, Orthodox divorce and remarriage.
8. Early and medieval Ireland: *Cáin Lánamna*, canon-law interaction, property classes, divorce, and later Anglo-Norman jurisdiction.
9. Germanic and northern Europe: Frankish law, Icelandic and other sagas, Scandinavian law, household alliances, divorce, concubinage.
10. Islamic legal worlds: Qur'an and hadith, legal schools, marriage contracts, mahr, talaq, khulʿ, judicial dissolution, Ottoman and other court practice.
11. Arabian Peninsula before and during early Islam: forms of union and the retrospective classifications preserved in Islamic sources.
12. China: Han through Tang and later imperial law, the “seven outs,” mutual divorce, remarriage, property, lineage, concubinage.
13. South Asia: Hindu legal traditions, regional customary law, caste, matriliny, Nayar Kerala, Islamic law, colonial codification.
14. Africa: regional kinship and marriage systems, bridewealth, matriliny and patriliny, Islamic and Christian interaction, colonial recognition.
15. Indigenous Americas and colonial regimes: local kinship systems, mission marriage, enslavement, racial classification, and state registration.
16. England and Wales: canon law, common law, clandestine marriage, poor law, wife sale, parliamentary divorce, civil registration, modern reform.
17. France: canon law, the 1792 Revolution, the Napoleonic Code, paternity suits, nineteenth-century reform.
18. Industrial and working-class marriage: wage dependence, informal unions, common-law myths, support law, domestic service, migration, and divorce cost.
19. Modern civil marriage: registration, no-fault divorce, same-sex marriage, welfare and tax policy, immigration, and privatization through contract.

## Prior anchors to preserve

The earlier discussion identified these sources and examples as important starting points:

- Susan Treggiari, Jane F. Gardner, Judith Evans Grubbs, and the Roman legal sources on marriage, dowry, divorce, concubinage, and widowhood.
- Roman *contubernium* and nonlegal unions as the strongest ancient test of marriage without property or free status.
- The Code of Hammurabi and Mesopotamian marriage and divorce documents.
- Egyptian marriage and divorce papyri.
- Athens and the Gortyn Code.
- *Cáin Lánamna* and Donnchadh Ó Corráin on early Irish marriage law and its interaction with canon law.
- The Tang Code, mutual divorce, the “seven outs” and “three non-expulsions,” and the gap between prescription and lived practice.
- Pre-Islamic Arabian forms of union as reported in early Islamic sources, together with the danger of treating later taxonomies as transparent ethnography.
- Qur'anic rules, hadith, juristic doctrine, contracts, and court practice on mahr, talaq, khulʿ, maintenance, children, and remarriage.
- Nayar Kerala and the maternal *taravad* as a challenge to household-centered European definitions.
- Augustine, Gratian, Lateran IV, papal decretals, and Trent's *Tametsi* in the development of Latin marriage law.
- E. P. Thompson on rough music and wife sale as socially intelligible but legally ineffective working-class divorce practices.
- Judean divorce around Jesus: the *get*, the schools of Hillel and Shammai, Josephus, Roman provincial context, and the disputed New Testament passages.
- Jack Goody on church, kinship, inheritance, and European family formation.
- Icelandic sagas, especially evidence about household competence, alliance, separation, and remarriage rather than romantic ideology.
- Henry Mayhew's *London Labour and the London Poor* and other “history from below” sources for cohabitation, casual labor, lodging, children, and informal household forms.
- Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's *Montaillou*, used cautiously alongside the Fournier Register and criticism of Ladurie's method.
- Friedrich Engels's argument connecting monogamous marriage, inheritance, private property, and women's subordination, treated as a historical theory to test rather than an unquestioned account.

## Method

### Separate rules from events

For every jurisdiction, maintain at least four columns:

1. normative law or theology;
2. legal procedure and enforcement;
3. documentary evidence of actual cases;
4. literary, ethnographic, or archaeological evidence of ordinary practice.

### Track the speaker and institutional interest

A bishop, jurist, husband, abandoned wife, slave owner, landlord, state registrar, village witness, and anthropologist do not describe the same object from interchangeable positions.

### Avoid civilizational shorthand

“Christian marriage,” “Islamic marriage,” “Chinese marriage,” and “African marriage” are research headings, not single systems. Specify period, place, school, court, class, and source.

### Do not romanticize customary alternatives

A dissoluble or informal union is not automatically freer for women, children, enslaved people, or poor partners. Flexibility can mean easy exit; it can also mean no support, no property claim, no recognized parentage, and no remedy against abandonment.

### Do not equate permanence with justice

An indissoluble bond can protect an economically vulnerable spouse against abandonment or trap that spouse with an abuser. A readily dissoluble union can preserve autonomy or allow the powerful partner to externalize every cost. The comparison must follow property, care, children, and coercion after exit.

## Immediate unresolved questions

1. How often did poor medieval Europeans use the canon-law rule that present consent could create a marriage without property or ceremony, and how could they prove it?
2. When did church recognition of marriages of enslaved or unfree people produce practical protection against sale or forced separation?
3. Which ancient and medieval systems gave an economically dependent spouse a stronger post-divorce claim than modern informal cohabitation does?
4. How did child residence and support work after nonmarital separation in different systems?
5. Which legal systems recognized unpaid care or lost earning capacity at dissolution?
6. Did easy divorce correlate with women's economic independence, as Rapoport argues for parts of medieval Islamic society, or did it usually magnify male power?
7. How did wage labor and the decline of household production alter what marriage did for working-class people?
8. When did governments begin making civil status, benefits, immigration, and taxation depend on registered marriage rather than household fact?
