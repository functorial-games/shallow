# Recovered earlier marriage-history thread

This file records specific conclusions and open problems from the earlier Shallow conversation so they are not flattened into a new generic bibliography.

## Earlier comparative thesis

The earlier discussion did **not** conclude that every non-Christian society had easy divorce or that Latin Christianity uniquely valued permanence.

Its more exact provisional claim was:

- Classical Roman marriage, Jewish divorce by *get*, Islamic *ṭalāq*, *khulʿ*, delegated divorce and judicial dissolution, Tang dismissal and mutual divorce, early Irish separation rules, Japanese forms of repudiation and divorce, the Gortyn Code, and Nayar unions all supplied routes by which a living couple could cease to be spouses or partners and form later unions.
- Latin Christian canon law built an unusually systematic doctrine in which a valid consummated marriage created a bond that the spouses could not dissolve in order to remarry. Augustine helped articulate the theological account; medieval canonists and papal decretals systematized it; ecclesiastical jurisdiction made it a legal institution; Trent reaffirmed the doctrine and, through *Tametsi*, imposed public canonical form where the decree was promulgated.
- **Hindu śāstric marriage was the major non-Christian permanence exception in the earlier comparison.** Some normative Sanskrit traditions treated marriage, especially for upper-caste women, as an enduring sacramental bond rather than an ordinary dissoluble contract. Actual regional, caste, customary, widow-remarriage, separation, and later legal practice varied sharply and must not be inferred from the normative texts alone.

The correct research question is therefore not “Did Christianity invent permanent marriage?” It is:

> Which traditions made marriage legally or ritually indissoluble, for whom, through what institution, and with what practical routes around the rule?

Routes around formal indissolubility can include nullity, separation without remarriage, abandonment, informal second unions, concubinage, migration, conversion, legislative divorce, widowhood, or simply nonenforcement.

## Earlier propertyless-marriage findings

The original question was whether marriage had much meaning for people who had no land, dowry, workshop, inheritance, or estate to transfer.

The strongest cases found were not symmetrical marriages between two perfectly propertyless free adults. They were relations in which legal status itself replaced property as the decisive fact.

### Roman enslaved *contubernium*

- Two enslaved people could form an enduring and socially recognized pair, but they lacked *conubium* and therefore could not contract Roman legal marriage.
- The union supplied little legal protection against sale or forced separation.
- Children followed the enslaved status of the mother.
- This case shows that denying legal marriage did not erase attachment, household cooperation, sex, reproduction, or kinship. It withheld enforceable claims over co-residence, children, succession, and family continuity.

### Enslaved and very low-property unions in Islamic legal settings

The earlier thread identified slave marriages and marriages with token or very small *mahr* as another strong test case.

Questions to preserve:

- Could an enslaved person consent independently, or did an owner control formation and dissolution?
- Who received or controlled *mahr*?
- Did marriage alter sexual exclusivity, maintenance, parentage, freedom status, or risk of sale?
- What happened when one spouse was free and the other enslaved?
- How much practical security did a nominal contract produce when neither spouse controlled a household or meaningful property?

This lane still needs a regional and school-specific source file. “Islamic slave marriage” cannot be treated as one rule across centuries.

### Poor European servants and laborers

The earlier discussion emphasized delayed marriage among servants and wage laborers who lacked the means to establish an independent household. Marriage could remain legally and religiously available while being materially postponed by service, housing, wages, poor-law settlement, employer rules, pregnancy, and the cost of household formation.

This is different from saying poor people did not marry. The issue is that the ability to create and maintain a separate household often functioned as an unstated economic precondition.

### Chinese brideprice and excluded men

Brideprice, household registration, lineage strategy, and unequal sex ratios could exclude poor men from ordinary marriage or delay it severely. That produces a different propertyless problem: not a legally unrecognized existing couple, but people unable to enter the socially central form at all.

### Cases where the evidence remained weak

The earlier search did **not** find clean, well-documented examples of marriages between two entirely propertyless free spouses in:

- classical Athens or Gortyn;
- early Irish law;
- Egyptian marriage contracts.

Those sources discuss a wider range of people than aristocratic dynasties, but property transfers, status, and household claims remain central to the surviving evidence. The absence of a clean zero-property case should be recorded rather than replaced with confident generalization.

## What happened to children after breakup

The earlier conversation treated child residence, support, lineage, and status as more revealing than the bare question “Was divorce allowed?”

### England and Wales: affiliation and poor-law proceedings

When unmarried parents separated or never formed a recognized household, mothers commonly performed daily care while parish and later magistrates' procedures attempted to identify and charge a putative father. Affiliation orders were partly child-support mechanisms and partly devices for shifting cost away from the parish.

Research questions:

- How often were orders obtained, enforced, evaded, or settled informally?
- Did the father's payment create any right to custody or contact?
- How did service, migration, imprisonment, and settlement law affect mothers and children?
- How did the system differ before and after the nineteenth-century reforms?

### Classical Islamic law: nonmarital children

The earlier synthesis stated provisionally that a child born outside a legally recognized marriage was generally attached to the mother and her kin unless paternal acknowledgment or another doctrine established lineage. This affected name, inheritance, maintenance, guardianship, and social status.

This requires precise treatment by legal school, period, slavery status, acknowledgment doctrine, and court practice. It should not be compressed into “fathers had no duties.”

### Nayar Kerala

Children belonged to the maternal *taravad*. A relationship's end therefore did not normally remove the child from the mother's corporate lineage in order to place the child in a divorced father's household. Maternal kin, property, and residence altered the entire meaning of “custody after divorce.”

### Roman slavery

The child followed the mother's enslaved status. A socially recognized father had no ordinary marital paternal power capable of preventing sale or separation. Here the law of status overwhelmed the family relation.

## Marriage as a material exchange and power structure

The earlier discussion used a deliberately stark model to test legal systems:

> Marriage can organize support, legal protection, property access, public status, and authority in exchange for claims over sexual exclusivity, reproduction, childrearing, domestic labor, and other work.

This is not a universal definition and should not be naturalized as a voluntary bargain. Under coverture, slavery, caste, unequal divorce rights, marital rape exemptions, wage exclusion, and inheritance law, the “exchange” could be coercively structured and the parties could have radically unequal power.

For each system, ask:

- What did a spouse gain that an unmarried partner lacked?
- What sexual, reproductive, domestic, and labor claims did the status grant the other spouse or household?
- Could either party refuse those claims without losing support, children, residence, reputation, or freedom?
- Did law recognize unpaid work or only ownership and wages?
- Which protections continued after separation?
- Did marriage protect a dependent partner from abandonment, or create the dependency that made abandonment dangerous?

This framework is especially useful for comparing coverture, dowry, *mahr*, bridewealth, community property, dower, widow rights, domestic service, and modern benefit systems. It should remain a set of questions, not a one-sentence origin theory.

## Rough music, wife sale, and popular separation

The earlier Thompson discussion was more specific than “poor English people sold wives.”

### Wife sale

- Public wife sales were legally ineffective but could operate as socially intelligible plebeian separations where formal divorce was prohibitively expensive or unavailable.
- Some cases appear to have been arranged by all three adults, including the wife's intended new partner; others involved coercion, humiliation, or retrospective justification.
- A halter, token payment, market setting, witnesses, and publicity could imitate a transfer while also announcing that the prior household had ended.
- Poor-law officers sometimes tolerated or participated because a publicly identified new partner simplified responsibility for maintenance, but this did not make the sale valid law.

### Rough music

Thompson's “rough music” involved community sanction against conduct understood to violate household and gender norms, including wife beating, adultery, publicly subordinated or “henpecked” husbands, violent or “scolding” wives, and norm-violating remarriages. It did not express one coherent emancipatory morality.

The custom declined as state policing, magistrates, privacy norms, and elite definitions of order displaced local public discipline. That shift could suppress communal cruelty while also removing a form of popular enforcement before poor people had equal access to formal remedies.

### Sources to preserve

- E. P. Thompson, “Rough Music” and “The Sale of Wives,” in *Customs in Common*.
- Thompson, “Rough Music Reconsidered.”
- Samuel Pyeatt Menefee, *Wives for Sale*.
- Martin Ingram and David Underdown on scolding, gender disorder, and popular discipline.
- Local newspapers, magistrates' records, poor-law records, and individual case studies needed to test Thompson's synthesis.

## *Montaillou*: desire, household, and marriage

The prior Shallow thread also treated *Montaillou* as evidence about falling in love, desire, sex, and the gap between household order and formal marriage.

Important figures and situations to recover:

- Béatrice de Planissoles and Barthélemy Amilhac;
- Pierre Maury and Bernadette d'Esquinath;
- Pierre Clergue's affairs, power, and coercion;
- migrant shepherds whose work and poverty delayed or destabilized marriage;
- relationships blocked by religion, class, household strategy, or community surveillance;
- older-woman/younger-man relations;
- love and sex occurring outside durable or legitimate marriage.

The source also belongs in the history-of-love lane because it helps test historical gender scripts. Medieval and early-modern learned traditions often stereotyped women as bodily, sexually voracious, and difficult to govern, while later Victorian respectability increasingly cast “good” women as comparatively passionless. Neither stereotype should be treated as behavior. Infer desire from particular testimony and conduct while accounting for interrogation, coercion, gossip, genre, and Ladurie's editorial method.
