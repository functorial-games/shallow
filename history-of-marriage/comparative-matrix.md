# Initial comparative matrix

This is a working map, not a finished statement of any legal system. Each row compresses centuries, regions, and disputes that require separate files. “Law” means the rule claimed by surviving sources; it does not establish prevalence or enforcement.

| Case | Formation and recognition | Dissolution and remarriage | Plural or secondary unions | Property and labor | Children and status | Propertyless or non-elite problem | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Old Babylonian law and the Code of Hammurabi | Marriage involved agreements between households and transfers such as bridewealth and dowry; surviving contracts matter alongside the law collection. | A husband could repudiate under rules that varied with children and fault; compensation or return of dowry could be due. A wife could seek exit in specified circumstances, but an unsuccessful accusation could expose her to severe punishment. Widows could remarry under conditions. | A second woman or slave concubine could enter the household under regulated circumstances, including infertility and illness. Status among women differed. | Dowry commonly remained connected to the wife and her children, while household control was strongly patriarchal. Rules address debts, support, and division on dissolution. | Rules distinguish children of wives, concubines, and enslaved women and regulate recognition and inheritance. | The code is preoccupied with property, status, and household hierarchy; poorer households appear indirectly through debt, service, and enslavement. | Hammurabi is a royal legal collection, not a transcript of ordinary practice. Compare contracts, letters, and court documents by period and city. |
| Classical Roman *iustum matrimonium* | No priest or state officer was generally required. Capacity to marry (*conubium*), consent, and continuing marital intention (*affectio maritalis*) distinguished lawful marriage. Public conduct, dowry, and documents could prove rather than necessarily create the union. | Marriage could end by mutual decision or unilateral repudiation. Divorced and widowed people ordinarily could remarry, subject to changing waiting periods, property penalties, and late-antique restrictions. | Only one legal wife at a time, but concubinage, slavery, prostitution, and sexual access produced a wider hierarchy of unions. | Spousal property could remain formally separate, with elaborate rules for dowry and gifts. Actual control depended on family authority, class, and period. | Lawful marriage shaped legitimacy, paternal power, household membership, citizenship, and inheritance. | Formal marriage mattered most visibly where status and succession were at stake, but common people also married and divorced. The surviving record is uneven. | “Roman marriage” changed over a millennium. Separate republican, classical, late-antique, provincial, and Justinianic law. |
| Roman enslaved unions, *contubernium* | Enslaved people lacked *conubium* and could not contract Roman legal marriage. Owners and communities might recognize durable pair bonds called *contubernium*. | No legal divorce was needed because Roman law did not recognize the marriage. Sale, manumission, owner command, or practical separation could end co-residence. | Owners could control partners' sexual and reproductive lives; exclusivity depended more on social practice than marital right. | Enslaved partners held no secure marital estate. Peculium and informal household goods did not create ordinary spousal property claims. | Children followed the mother's enslaved status; the father acquired no ordinary marital or paternal rights through the union. Families could be separated by sale. | This is the clearest case in which a relationship could be socially marriage-like while the legal package was withheld because the partners lacked freedom and property-bearing status. | Do not infer emotional or household insignificance from legal nonrecognition. Evidence is largely written by owners and jurists. |
| Early Irish *Cáin Lánamna* | The tract describes several unions differentiated partly by the relative property contributions and status of the partners, rather than one undifferentiated marriage form. | Grounds for separation and division of property are specified. Fault, contributions, labor, and circumstances affect settlement. Remarriage and secondary unions were possible in the broader legal material. | Sources recognize principal and secondary unions and a social world not reducible to later Latin monogamous canon law. | Household goods, land, livestock, products, and labor are central. The tract attempts to allocate gains and losses by contribution and fault. | Kinship, status, fosterage, and property affect children, though the whole child regime requires other tracts. | Different property classes reveal more than elite dowry alone, but genuinely propertyless unions remain difficult to reconstruct. | The surviving tract was written in a Christian learned environment. Its date, layers, relation to practice, and interaction with Roman and canon law are disputed. |
| High-medieval Latin canon law | By the mature consent doctrine, the spouses' valid present consent could create marriage; consummation affected indissolubility in important doctrinal formulations. Publicity, banns, witnesses, and later canonical form addressed proof and clandestinity. | A valid consummated marriage was not dissolved so that the spouses could remarry. Courts could declare nullity or permit separation from bed and board. Death ended the bond. | Monogamy was mandatory; bigamy and many sexual alternatives were prohibited, although concubinage and clandestine unions persisted. | Canon law governed formation and bond; secular and local systems often governed dowry, dower, community property, inheritance, support, and household goods. | Legitimacy and marriage strongly affected inheritance and status, although canon law developed protections and legitimation doctrines. | In principle even poor and unfree people could marry by consent, and later canon law denied masters a power to invalidate such marriages. In practice proof, household formation, lordship, and sale could frustrate the right. | Doctrine developed over centuries. Do not project Trent backward or assume church-court enforcement was uniform. |
| Medieval Islamic urban societies, with late-medieval Cairo, Damascus, and Jerusalem as one studied case | Marriage was a contract involving consent, witnesses under most schools, and an obligatory *mahr* claim. Written contracts could add stipulations. | A husband possessed unilateral *talaq* powers; wives could pursue *khulʿ*, delegated divorce, contractual remedies, or judicial dissolution under school- and period-specific rules. Both sexes commonly remarried in some documented cities. | A man could marry multiple wives within juristic limits and could have enslaved concubines; actual prevalence varied greatly. | Classical doctrine generally kept spouses' property separate. *Mahr*, dowry, wages, maintenance, and negotiated settlements gave some women resources at dissolution. | Paternity, lineage, custody, guardianship, maintenance, and inheritance followed distinct rules; custody of young children and legal guardianship could be divided. | Yossef Rapoport argues that female wage labor, dowries, separate property, and monetized marriage helped make divorce frequent and often initiated by women in selected late-medieval cities. This cannot be generalized to all Islamic societies. | Distinguish Qur'an, hadith, juristic doctrine, contract clauses, court practice, region, school, slavery, and class. “Islamic marriage law” is not one unchanging code. |
| Early medieval and Tang China | Marriage ordinarily joined lineages and involved family negotiation, ritual, and property transfers. The Tang Code and related sources regulated valid unions and household hierarchy. | A husband could expel a wife under the “seven outs,” constrained by the “three non-expulsions”; mutual divorce (*he li*) and other departures also existed. Evidence outside the code shows women initiating or effecting separation in circumstances not captured by a simple rule list. Remarriage was possible. | One principal wife coexisted with concubines and other dependent women; status among them and among their children mattered. | Dowry, betrothal gifts, household property, and lineage claims structured marriage. Women's practical control differed by period and class. | Marriage located children within a patrilineal household and affected rank and inheritance; the principal wife/concubine distinction mattered. | Divorce and remarriage appear beyond elites, but legal and literary evidence still overrepresents households worth recording. | The “seven outs” are often repeated as if they exhausted practice. Separate ritual prescription, Tang criminal/administrative law, contracts, epitaphs, and actual cases. |
| Nayar Kerala before and during colonial transformation | Some Nayar groups organized residence and property through the matrilineal *taravad*. Relations often described as *sambandham* did not necessarily create a European-style co-resident conjugal household. Rituals and practices varied by subgroup and period. | A relationship could often be ended without a state divorce proceeding; partners might form later unions. Colonial law increasingly demanded categorical marriage status. | Historical reports describe women or men having more than one partner in some settings, but prevalence and interpretation are heavily disputed. | Corporate matrilineal property belonged to the *taravad*; a woman's brother and maternal kin could be more central to property and household authority than a husband. | Children belonged to the maternal lineage rather than becoming heirs in the European paternal household model. | This case directly challenges the assumption that marriage must transfer a woman and children into a husband's property-bearing household. | Colonial officials and early anthropologists often exoticized Nayar sexuality. Separate regional practice, caste rank, time, source bias, and twentieth-century reform. |
| Second Temple and early rabbinic Jewish divorce | Marriage involved betrothal and marriage stages, property obligations later associated with the *ketubah*, kin, and religious law. Roman provincial institutions formed part of the environment in Judea. | A husband normally effected divorce by delivering a *get*. Sources debate valid grounds: the schools of Shammai and Hillel are represented as narrower and broader, with Akiva broader still. Women's routes to compel or obtain divorce require separate treatment. Remarriage generally existed, subject to restrictions. | Jewish law permitted polygyny in antiquity and parts of the medieval world; practice varied. | Marriage payments, maintenance, and settlement obligations attempted to regulate abandonment and dissolution. | Parentage, lineage, inheritance, and forbidden unions were central. | Documentary evidence such as Judean Desert marriage and divorce texts can correct a purely doctrinal picture, but surviving cases are few. | New Testament sayings, Josephus, Mishnah, later Talmud, and Roman law come from different genres and dates. Do not collapse them into a single “Jewish view in Jesus's time.” |
| English working-class wife sale, eighteenth and nineteenth centuries | Couples were legally married under English law, often without realistic access to parliamentary divorce. A public “sale” sometimes imitated market transfer with a halter, payment, witnesses, and community notice. | The sale did not legally dissolve the marriage. It could function socially as a consensual separation and transfer to a new partner, but coercive and contested cases also occurred. Remarriage remained legally dangerous while the spouse lived. | Legal monogamy; subsequent unions risked bigamy or remained informal. | Coverture and poverty made legal divorce and separate household formation difficult. A token payment and public ritual supplied community legibility, not a valid property conveyance of a wife. | Poor-law liability, legitimacy, support, and parish settlement could remain tied to the legal marriage despite the social breakup. | This is evidence that people excluded by the price of formal divorce built a recognizable substitute. It does not show that they believed wives were ordinary chattels or that every sale was consensual. | E. P. Thompson emphasized custom and community meaning. His account must be checked against court records, newspapers, local studies, and women's positions. |
| Revolutionary and nineteenth-century France | Civil marriage became a state institution during the Revolution. Formation, family authority, property, and registration changed repeatedly through revolution, empire, restoration, and republic. | The 1792 law allowed comparatively liberal divorce, including mutual consent. The 1804 Civil Code restricted it; divorce was abolished in 1816 and restored in 1884 under narrower terms. | Civil monogamy. Informal unions persisted. | The Civil Code structured marital authority and property while sharply subordinating married women in important respects. | Legitimate and nonmarital children had different claims. For much of the nineteenth century, legal barriers to paternity investigation left unmarried mothers and children exposed. | Formal marriage could be economically inaccessible or undesirable, but nonmarital partners lacked many protections. The state's refusal to recognize paternity shifted costs to women, children, kin, and charity. | France is a sequence of regimes, not a single Napoleonic rule. Track exact statute, date, gender, class, and colonial application. |
