# Annotated bibliography

This bibliography is a starting archive. It separates primary or near-primary source collections from later interpretation and notes what each source can and cannot establish. A law code proves that a rule was formulated and preserved; it does not by itself prove how common the conduct was, how courts enforced the rule, or how poor people experienced it.

## Comparative and methodological works

### Jack Goody — *The Development of the Family and Marriage in Europe* (1983)

- Use: major comparative argument that the Western Church's restrictions on close-kin marriage, adoption, concubinage, divorce, remarriage, and other heir-producing arrangements affected property transmission and the development of European kinship.
- Question to test: how much institutional self-interest and property accumulation explain ecclesiastical marriage policy, and how much the argument underestimates theology, local variation, and resistance.
- Caution: influential and deliberately broad; later regional studies complicate a single causal account.

### Jack Goody — *The Oriental, the Ancient and the Primitive* (1990)

- Use: comparative challenge to treating modern Western marriage, family, and domestic organization as universal or as the endpoint of a single evolutionary sequence.
- Caution: use regional scholarship rather than relying on its large civilizational contrasts.

### G. Robina Quale — *A History of Marriage Systems* (1988)

- Use: broad survey and typology useful for locating systems and older scholarship.
- Caution: a map, not the final authority for any region.

### Mary Ann Glendon, ed. — *International Encyclopedia of Comparative Law, Volume IV: Persons and Family*

- Use: comparative legal categories, especially formation, status, property, parentage, and dissolution.
- Caution: legal doctrine and national summaries require updating and comparison with social history.

### Friedrich Engels — *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884)

- Use: explicit theory linking monogamous marriage, inheritance, private property, class society, and women's subordination.
- Research value: supplies hypotheses about property and household power that can be tested against later anthropology and history.
- Caution: built partly on nineteenth-century evolutionary anthropology, especially Lewis Henry Morgan. Do not treat its stages as established history.

### Claude Lévi-Strauss — *The Elementary Structures of Kinship*

- Use: marriage as exchange and alliance among kin groups; useful for seeing why a couple-centered model misses the wider institution.
- Caution: structural models can suppress history, material inequality, individual experience, and relations that do not fit an exchange schema.

## Mesopotamia

### Martha Roth, trans. — *Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor*

- Use: modern scholarly translations of the Code of Ur-Namma, Laws of Eshnunna, Code of Hammurabi, Middle Assyrian Laws, and related collections.
- Relevant topics: betrothal, bridewealth, dowry, divorce, widowhood, debt, slavery, children, inheritance, and household authority.
- Caution: law collections are not comprehensive modern codes and may have pedagogical, royal, or ideological functions.

### Raymond Westbrook — *Old Babylonian Marriage Law* (1988)

- Use: contracts, transfers, formation, dissolution, and the relationship between legal texts and actual documents.
- Caution: “Babylonian marriage” varied by city, century, status, and source type.

### Raymond Westbrook, ed. — *A History of Ancient Near Eastern Law* (2003)

- Use: broad regional reference with legal institutions placed in chronological context.
- Caution: consult the underlying documents for claims about ordinary practice.

## Egypt

### Jane Rowlandson, ed. — *Women and Society in Greek and Roman Egypt: A Sourcebook*

- Use: papyri on marriage contracts, divorce, dowry, work, household authority, property, and children.
- Strength: documentary evidence from actual transactions and disputes rather than only literary ideals.
- Caution: surviving papyri are regionally and socially uneven.

### Roger S. Bagnall — “Church, State and Divorce in Late Roman Egypt”

- Use: tests claims about immediate Christian transformation by comparing ecclesiastical positions, imperial law, and Egyptian practice.
- Caution: late Roman Egypt cannot stand for the entire empire.

### Amira El-Azhary Sonbol, ed. — *Women, the Family, and Divorce Laws in Islamic History* (1996)

- Use: regional studies of court practice and family law, including Egypt and Ottoman settings.
- Caution: chapters differ in period, source base, and legal school.

## Greece

### The Gortyn Code

- Use: unusually extensive inscription from fifth-century BCE Crete concerning marriage, divorce, property, inheritance, adoption, sexual offenses, enslaved people, and children.
- Research question: how its provisions differ from Athenian institutions and from later generalizations about “Greek marriage.”
- Caution: Gortyn is one polis with its own status system.

### Sarah B. Pomeroy — *Goddesses, Whores, Wives, and Slaves*

- Use: foundational synthesis of women's legal and social positions in Greek and Roman worlds.
- Caution: supplement with newer work and local documentary studies.

### Cheryl Anne Cox — *Household Interests: Property, Marriage Strategies, and Family Dynamics in Ancient Athens*

- Use: marriage as household strategy, property transmission, kinship, and demographic behavior.
- Caution: Athenian citizen households are not the whole Greek world and sources privilege male citizens.

## Rome and late antiquity

### Susan Treggiari — *Roman Marriage: Iusti Coniuges from the Time of Cicero to the Time of Ulpian* (1991)

- Use: central study of valid Roman marriage, consent, marital intention, dowry, divorce, social practice, and elite evidence in the late Republic and Principate.
- Caution: the source base is richest for citizens and elites; do not project the classical settlement unchanged into late antiquity.

### Beryl Rawson, ed. — *Marriage, Divorce, and Children in Ancient Rome* (1991)

- Use: essays on divorce frequency, children, family strategy, law, demography, and ordinary people.
- Particularly relevant: Susan Treggiari on Roman divorce and Iiro Kajanto's work on divorce among common people.

### Judith Evans Grubbs — *Women and the Law in the Roman Empire: A Sourcebook on Marriage, Divorce and Widowhood* (2002)

- Use: translated legal and documentary sources from Augustus through late antiquity, with sections on lawful marriage, prohibited and nonlegal unions, divorce, dowry, widows, guardianship, Egypt, and the Near East.
- Open record: https://archive.org/details/womenlawinromane0000grub.
- Strength: allows Shallow to distinguish juristic rules, imperial constitutions, petitions, contracts, and provincial documents.

### Judith Evans Grubbs — *Law and Family in Late Antiquity: The Emperor Constantine's Marriage Legislation* (1995)

- Use: Christian influence, continuity with Roman law, and the political and social purposes of Constantine's family legislation.
- Caution: Christianization was neither instantaneous nor unidirectional.

### Jane F. Gardner — *Women in Roman Law and Society*

- Use: legal capacity, guardianship, property, marriage, dowry, divorce, motherhood, and status.

### Suzanne Dixon — *The Roman Family* (1992)

- Use: household relations and the gap between legal form and social experience.

### Mathew Kuefler — “The Marriage Revolution in Late Antiquity: The Theodosian Code and Later Roman Marriage Law”

- Use: fourth- and fifth-century changes in marriage choice, payments, adultery, divorce, remarriage, inheritance, enslaved people, soldiers, and clergy.
- Link: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0363199007304424.
- Caution: the Theodosian Code is a selective compilation; legislation can reveal conflict as much as compliance.

### Ruth Mazo Karras — *Unmarriages: Women, Men, and Sexual Unions in the Middle Ages* (2012)

- Use: concubinage, clerical partners, unequal-status unions, interfaith unions, clandestine unions, and long-term relationships outside formal marriage from late antiquity through the Middle Ages.
- Link: https://www.pennpress.org/9780812222982/unmarriages/.
- Central value: asks what marriage meant when poverty, slavery, religious status, or rank prevented the usual public and property arrangements.
- Caution: the evidence cannot support precise prevalence estimates.

## Jewish law and Judea

### Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 24:1–4 and related marriage, inheritance, levirate, and sexual laws

- Use: textual background for a written bill of divorce, remarriage restrictions, household succession, and later interpretation.
- Caution: a text's date, redaction, genre, and later legal reception must be distinguished from social practice in any particular period.

### Mishnah: tractates *Gittin*, *Ketubot*, *Yevamot*, and *Kiddushin*

- Use: divorce documents, marital payments and duties, levirate marriage, prohibited unions, betrothal, and status.
- Caution: compiled around 200 CE; it cannot simply be read back as a complete description of first-century Judea.

### The Judean Desert documents, including the Babatha archive

- Use: actual marriage contracts, property arrangements, guardianship, litigation, and provincial interaction among Jewish, local, and Roman legal forms.
- Caution: small and exceptional archives survive because of unusual preservation.

### Josephus — *Jewish Antiquities* and *Life*

- Use: elite first-century representations of marriage and divorce, including Josephus's own marital history.
- Caution: autobiographical and apologetic purposes shape the account.

### Shaye J. D. Cohen — work on Jewish identity, descent, conversion, and marriage in antiquity

- Use: transformation of boundaries and status rather than an assumption of timeless matrilineal descent.

### Ze'ev W. Falk — *Jewish Matrimonial Law in the Middle Ages*

- Use: formation, property, polygyny, divorce, communal regulation, and interaction with surrounding legal systems.

## Latin Christianity and medieval Europe

### Augustine — *On the Good of Marriage*, *On Adulterous Marriages*, and related works

- Use: goods of marriage, fidelity, offspring, sacramental bond, divorce, remarriage, and sexual ethics in late-antique Latin Christianity.
- Caution: theological argument, not a code or direct description of household practice.

### Gratian — *Decretum*, especially Causae 27–36

- Use: twelfth-century synthesis of discordant authorities on betrothal, consent, consummation, impediments, unfree spouses, separation, and marriage litigation.
- Caution: the work contains layers and competing authorities; later decretals altered and clarified the law.

### Fourth Lateran Council (1215), canon 51

- Use: publication of marriages, impediments, and the attempt to reduce clandestine and prohibited unions.
- Caution: it did not create the basic consent doctrine, nor did it make priestly ceremony universally constitutive of marriage.

### Gregory IX — *Liber Extra*, Book 4

- Use: official thirteenth-century collection of papal decretals on marriage formation, impediments, legitimacy, unfree spouses, and dissolution questions.

### Council of Trent — Session 24, decree *Tametsi* (1563)

- Use: sacramental doctrine and the requirement, where promulgated, that Catholics marry before the parish priest or delegated priest and witnesses for validity.
- Caution: promulgation and reception varied; it should not be projected backward onto all medieval marriage.

### James A. Brundage — *Law, Sex, and Christian Society in Medieval Europe* (1987)

- Use: broad legal and theological history of sex, marriage, clerical discipline, courts, and canon law.
- Caution: broad synthesis; verify regional and chronological claims against newer work.

### Charles Donahue Jr. — *Law, Marriage, and Society in the Later Middle Ages* (2007)

- Use: quantitative and qualitative comparison of marriage litigation in York, Ely, Paris, Cambrai, and Brussels.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/law-marriage-and-society-in-the-later-middle-ages/E1596BA0C61049096673C8014F029BD4.
- Central value: shows that courts applying a shared canon law produced different case patterns and results under different property and social regimes.

### Philip L. Reynolds — *Marriage in the Western Church: The Christianization of Marriage During the Patristic and Early Medieval Periods*

- Use: development before the high-medieval sacramental and canonical synthesis.

### Philip L. Reynolds and John Witte Jr., eds. — *To Have and to Hold: Marrying and Its Documentation in Western Christendom, 400–1600* (2007)

- Use: marriage documents and proof from Rome, North Africa, Frankish regions, southern France, England, Ireland, Iceland, Florence, Douai, and Geneva.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/to-have-and-to-hold/CB8BB3AC5BF432628C519FF78A4636E3.
- Central value: compares what created a marriage with what documented and proved one.

### Sara McDougall — “Marriage: Law and Practice,” in *The Cambridge History of Medieval Canon Law*

- Use: current synthesis of doctrine, litigation, social practice, and unresolved historiography.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-history-of-medieval-canon-law/marriage-law-and-practice/FA86E39AA389A9415B132A7E5D033F14.

### David d'Avray — work on medieval marriage, symbolism, and slave marriage

- Use: gradual incorporation of unfree marriages into canon law and the long distance between legal recognition and practical protection.
- Open article on slavery and marriage: http://data.rg.mpg.de/rechtsgeschichte/rg20_347d-avray.pdf.

### Alice Rio — *Slavery After Rome, 500–1100*

- Use: unfreedom, sale, manumission, household and estate slavery, mixed-status unions, children, and the use of legal status as leverage.
- Caution: rejects a single smooth transition from Roman slavery to medieval serfdom.

## Early Ireland

### *Cáin Lánamna* (“The Law of Couples”)

- Edition and translation: Rudolf Thurneysen in D. A. Binchy, ed., *Studies in Early Irish Law* (1936).
- Use: several forms of union differentiated by property contribution and status; division of household gains; labor; fault; separation.
- Caution: difficult learned text preserved in a Christian legal culture; date, layers, and relationship to practice require specialist work.

### Donnchadh Ó Corráin — “Marriage in Early Ireland”

- Open text: https://celt.ucc.ie/marriage_ei.html.
- Use: *Cáin Lánamna*, inheritance strategies, Roman and canon-law influence, divorce, secondary unions, and the later clash with reforming ecclesiastical law.
- Strength: identifies where apparently archaic Irish rules were entangled with late Roman and biblical legal traditions.

### Fergus Kelly — *A Guide to Early Irish Law*

- Use: orientation to status, kinship, property, marriage, fosterage, and the legal tracts.
- Caution: return to editions and current specialist debate for contested points.

## Iceland and northern Europe

### The Icelandic family sagas and *Sturlunga saga*

- Use: narrated evidence about household competence, courtship, alliance, fosterage, inheritance, seasonal absence, concubinage, separation, widowhood, and remarriage.
- Caution: literary works written after many events they depict; not transparent transcripts of Viking-age custom.

### Grágás: the laws of the Icelandic Commonwealth

- Use: legal rules on marriage, betrothal, property, sexuality, children, and household obligations.
- Caution: manuscript date, compilation, and the relation between written rule and saga practice matter.

### Agnes S. Arnórsdóttir — *Property and Virginity: The Christianization of Marriage in Medieval Iceland, 1200–1600*

- Use: church law, property, marriage contracts, sexual status, and regional adaptation.

### Jenny Jochens — *Women in Old Norse Society*

- Use: marriage, divorce, property, sexuality, and women's action across law and saga.

## Islamic legal worlds

### Qur'an: especially 2:226–241, 4:3–35, 4:127–130, 33:49, 58:1–4, and 65:1–7

- Use: marriage limits, mahr, conflict, separation, waiting periods, maintenance, repudiation, and remarriage.
- Caution: interpretation depends on language, chronology, exegesis, hadith, legal school, and historical practice.

### Major hadith collections and early legal works

- Use: reports used by jurists to construct rules of consent, guardianship, mahr, sexual access, maintenance, divorce, *khulʿ*, and custody.
- Caution: hadith criticism, legal selection, and differences among schools must remain visible.

### Kecia Ali — *Marriage and Slavery in Early Islam* (2010)

- Use: formation of juristic marriage doctrine in relation to ownership, sexual access, gender hierarchy, and slavery.
- Central value: prevents modern romantic or contractarian language from hiding the legal structure of premodern doctrine.

### Kecia Ali — *Sexual Ethics and Islam*

- Use: modern ethical argument engaging classical jurisprudence, consent, marriage, divorce, and gender.
- Caution: normative intervention as well as history.

### Yossef Rapoport — *Marriage, Money and Divorce in Medieval Islamic Society* (2005)

- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/marriage-money-and-divorce-in-medieval-islamic-society/35A5E65936B2522D05DB7ED7C3149D77.
- Use: late-medieval Cairo, Damascus, and Jerusalem; dowry, women's wage labor, separate property, monetized marriage, divorce settlements, and high divorce/remarriage.
- Central thesis to test: women's economic activity and property helped make divorce ordinary and sometimes female-initiated despite patriarchal juristic doctrine.
- Caution: specific urban societies and source archives; not a claim about all Muslim societies.

### Judith E. Tucker — *In the House of the Law: Gender and Islamic Law in Ottoman Syria and Palestine*

- Use: court practice, juristic doctrine, maintenance, marriage, divorce, sexuality, and women's litigation.

### Leslie Peirce — *Morality Tales: Law and Gender in the Ottoman Court of Aintab*

- Use: local court use, reputation, community, sex, marriage, and gender in an Ottoman town.

### Wael B. Hallaq — works on Islamic legal authority and change

- Use: how doctrine, jurists, judges, custom, fatwas, and political authority interacted; prevents treating a modern statute book as the model for premodern law.

## China

### Wallace Johnson, trans. — *The T'ang Code*

- Use: authoritative translation of the Tang legal code and commentary.
- Relevant topics: household hierarchy, valid and prohibited marriages, status, divorce-related offenses, concubinage, and kinship.
- Caution: a penal-administrative code does not exhaust civil practice or women's lived options.

### Patricia Buckley Ebrey — *The Inner Quarters: Marriage and the Lives of Chinese Women in the Sung Period*

- Use: marriage formation, dowry, patrilocal household, widowhood, remarriage, concubinage, and women's strategies.
- Caution: Song period, not a timeless Chinese pattern.

### Patricia Buckley Ebrey — *Women and the Family in Chinese History*

- Use: essays across periods on kinship, ritual, property, gender, and family change.

### “Divorce and the Divorced Woman in Early Medieval China (First Through Sixth Century)”

- Harvard dissertation: https://nrs.harvard.edu/urn-3:HUL.InstRepos:33493331.
- Use: legal and ritual rules compared with historical writing, epitaphs, literature, and actual cases; argues divorce and remarriage were more varied and less stigmatized than later prescriptions imply.
- Caution: dissertation's period precedes the Tang and should not be merged with later imperial norms.

### Kathryn Bernhardt — *Women and Property in China, 960–1949*

- Use: dowry, inheritance, widowhood, daughters, household property, and long-run legal change.

## South Asia and Kerala

### Kathleen Gough — studies of Nayar kinship and marriage

- Use: matrilineal *taravad*, residence, property, *sambandham*, colonial change, and the problem of applying a European conjugal-household definition.
- Caution: mid-twentieth-century anthropological frameworks and retrospective reconstruction require comparison with historians and local scholarship.

### K. M. Kapadia — *Marriage and Family in India*

- Use: comparative regional and caste variation as an older survey.
- Caution: categories and generalizations require revision through newer gender and legal history.

### Janaki Nair and other historians of colonial family-law reform in Kerala

- Use: how colonial courts, social reform, caste politics, inheritance, and modern statutes transformed matrilineal systems.

### Hindu legal texts and their commentarial traditions

- Primary orientation: *Dharmasūtras*, *Dharmaśāstras*, regional digests, inscriptions, and actual court or household documents.
- Caution: Sanskrit normative texts do not directly report practice across region, caste, century, or political order.

## England, Wales, and working-class practice

### R. H. Helmholz — *Marriage Litigation in Medieval England*

- Use: church courts, clandestine marriage, proof, promises, sex, annulment, and separation.

### Lawrence Stone — *Road to Divorce: England 1530–1987*

- Use: long institutional history of separation, parliamentary divorce, ecclesiastical jurisdiction, civil divorce, and class barriers.
- Caution: some of Stone's broad social interpretations have been heavily debated.

### E. P. Thompson — “Rough Music” and “The Sale of Wives,” in *Customs in Common* (1991)

- Use: community ritual, customary legitimacy, public separation, class, and the socially intelligible wife sale as an improvised substitute where legal divorce was inaccessible.
- Caution: wife sale was not legal divorce; cases differed in consent and coercion. Compare Thompson with local records and later scholarship.

### Samuel Pyeatt Menefee — *Wives for Sale: An Ethnographic Study of British Popular Divorce*

- Use: case collection and ritual variation.
- Caution: “popular divorce” describes social function, not legal validity or uniform consent.

### Henry Mayhew — *London Labour and the London Poor*

- Use: near-contemporary interviews and descriptions of casual labor, lodging, cohabitation, children, desertion, sexuality, and household survival in nineteenth-century London.
- Caution: Mayhew selected, edited, and framed testimony; sensational and moralizing elements require corroboration.

### Poor-law and affiliation records

- Use: how parishes and courts assigned support for nonmarital children and shifted costs among mothers, alleged fathers, kin, and local authorities.
- Research need: distinguish bastardy examinations, affiliation orders, settlement law, and later child-support statutes by date.

### Ginger Frost — work on Victorian and Edwardian couples living outside legal marriage

- Use: bigamy, separated spouses, cohabitation, illegitimacy, reputation, and the practical barriers to divorce.

## France

### French divorce laws of 1792, the Civil Code of 1804, the abolition of divorce in 1816, and the Naquet Law of 1884

- Use: a clear sequence showing marriage shifting among revolutionary civil contract, restrictive imperial family order, restoration-era indissolubility, and republican divorce.
- Caution: read exact statutory texts and procedural access; formal availability did not make divorce affordable or equal.

### Rachel G. Fuchs — work on poor women, illegitimacy, paternity, and welfare in nineteenth-century France

- Use: how restrictions on paternity suits and weak support rights distributed the cost of nonmarital children.

### Suzanne Desan — *The Family on Trial in Revolutionary France*

- Use: revolutionary law, divorce, inheritance, family conflict, citizenship, and litigants' use of new institutions.

### Jean-Louis Halpérin and other legal historians of the French family

- Use: doctrinal and institutional chronology beyond a simple “Napoleonic patriarchy” summary.

## Microhistory and history from below

### Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie — *Montaillou: The Promised Land of Error*

- Use: household (*ostal/domus*), dowry, inheritance, servants, concubinage, clerical partners, marriage, sex, and village networks in early-fourteenth-century Ariège.
- Source base: the inquisition register of Jacques Fournier.
- Caution: testimony was produced under interrogation; Ladurie's selection, paraphrase, gender analysis, and claims about a stable peasant mentality have been criticized. Read against the register and later critiques.

### The Register of Jacques Fournier

- Use: direct source for statements attributed to villagers, though mediated by interrogation, translation, scribal recording, and institutional pressure.

### Carlo Ginzburg and microhistorical method

- Use: disciplined inference from exceptional records while keeping source production and evidentiary limits explicit.

## Slavery, colonialism, and marriage

### Herman L. Bennett — *Africans in Colonial Mexico: Absolutism, Christianity, and Afro-Creole Consciousness, 1570–1640*

- Use: enslaved Africans' marriages, church jurisdiction, community, kinship, and contests with owners in New Spain.

### Brenda E. Stevenson — *Life in Black and White: Family and Community in the Slave South*

- Use: enslaved family formation, sale, forced separation, gender, and post-emancipation transitions.

### Tera W. Hunter — *Bound in Wedlock: Slave and Free Black Marriage in the Nineteenth Century*

- Use: unions under slavery, emancipation-era legalization, federal and state intervention, gender conflict, labor, and the costs and benefits of legal marriage.
- Central value: legal recognition could protect claims while also imposing state-defined duties and surveillance.

### Katherine M. Franke — *Wedlocked: The Perils of Marriage Equality*

- Use: post-emancipation marriage policy and the coercive side of compulsory marital normalization; comparison with modern marriage-equality politics.
- Caution: normative legal argument as well as history.

## Bibliographic rules for future additions

Every regional file should include:

1. at least one primary law or ritual text;
2. at least one documentary collection of actual contracts, cases, or household records;
3. a study of property and labor;
4. a study of children, parentage, or inheritance;
5. a study of poor, enslaved, unfree, migrant, or otherwise non-elite people;
6. a study of dissolution and post-separation consequences;
7. explicit notes on date, place, class, gender, source production, and what cannot be inferred.

Avoid bibliography by prestige alone. The archive should preserve disagreements and sources that can falsify the folder's provisional theses.
