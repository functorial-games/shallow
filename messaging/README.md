# The exciting messaging phase

This is about the period when a person is not merely willing to answer but actively hopes that one particular person has written.

The target is **partner-specific anticipation**, not app engagement. A streak, notification, variable reward, or unread badge can make somebody check an app. It cannot by itself make the user glad that *this person* has spoken.

## Working thesis

A message becomes exciting when it does at least two of these things at once:

1. **It recognizes something particular.** The sender noticed, remembered, or reconsidered something that could not have been addressed to just anybody.
2. **It leaves a live continuation.** The message completes a thought but opens another one. It gives the recipient something worth returning to, not an obligation to acknowledge receipt.
3. **It reveals the sender.** The recipient learns how this person notices, judges, remembers, jokes, or changes their mind—not merely another fact for a profile.
4. **It changes the shared world.** A song, story, disagreement, place, private joke, possible outing, or unfinished question now belongs partly to both people.
5. **It implies a future.** The conversation is not an isolated performance. Each person has reason to believe there will be another exchange or encounter in which this thread may matter.

The strongest excitement may come from a simple combination:

> You remembered me accurately, you brought me something I could not have predicted, and what I say next might matter to you.

That is different from “high engagement.” It is also different from maximal disclosure, constant availability, or rapid escalation.

## Dostoevsky's useful scene

In *The Idiot*, Part III, Chapter VIII, Aglaya tells Prince Myshkin that she intends to tell him everything, demands that he conceal nothing in return, and says that she wants at least one person she can address as she addresses herself.

The surrounding scene matters. She does not merely want a listener. She wants:

- relief from having to package herself for her family;
- reciprocal candor rather than one-sided confession;
- a person who will take her thought seriously;
- an ally in a possible future outside her current role;
- freedom to move among absurdity, moral ambition, anger, dependence, friendship, and love without having the whole exchange reduced to courtship.

This suggests a better goal for Shallow than “ask deep questions.” The goal is to help two people reach the point where they can think in front of each other without continually returning to self-presentation.

## Four processes worth distinguishing

### 1. Disclosure

One person makes some part of their experience available. Facts alone can count, but feelings, interpretations, embarrassments, judgments, and unfinished thoughts usually carry more risk and more information about the person.

Disclosure is not automatically intimacy. Premature or compulsory disclosure can feel like extraction. An app can manufacture the *appearance* of depth by asking increasingly invasive questions while neither person has earned the other's trust.

### 2. Responsiveness

The reply shows that the disclosure was understood well enough to affect the next turn. A responsive message need not agree, praise, comfort, or imitate therapy. It can disagree sharply while still proving that the sender grasped the actual claim.

A good follow-up question often works because it is evidence of attention. But question count is a crude proxy. The question “What do you do for fun?” after somebody has just told a complicated story is less responsive than a declarative reply that catches the story's important turn.

Research starting points:

- Laurenceau, Barrett, and Pietromonaco, “Intimacy as an Interpersonal Process” (1998): disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness in daily interactions. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1238.
- Jiang, Bazarova, and Hancock, “From Perception to Behavior: Disclosure Reciprocity and the Intensification of Intimacy in Computer-Mediated Communication” (2013): text communication can intensify how intimate a disclosure seems and therefore intensify reciprocal disclosure. https://socialmedialab.sites.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj22976/files/media/file/jiang-cr-from-perception.pdf.
- Huang et al., “It Doesn't Hurt to Ask: Question-Asking Increases Liking” (2017): experiments found that more questions, especially follow-ups, increased perceived responsiveness and liking. A later reanalysis disputed parts of the speed-dating result, so use the experiments as a clue about responsiveness, not a universal rule or product KPI. ### 3. Reciprocity

Reciprocity does not require symmetrical biographies or equal word counts. It means that one person is not indefinitely asked to become visible while the other remains an evaluator.

The useful pattern is often:

1. I risk a little.
2. You show that you received it.
3. You place something of your own beside it.
4. I can now answer at a slightly different level.

This can spiral toward intimacy. In text, it can also spiral too fast because a written disclosure may look more deliberate and intimate than the sender intended. Shallow should therefore support reciprocity without automatically ratcheting every exchange toward greater vulnerability.

### 4. Generativity

The best conversations do not simply reveal two pre-existing profiles. They produce thoughts, jokes, plans, distinctions, and memories that neither person had before the exchange.

This is where excitement differs from compatibility screening. Screening asks, “Does this person fit what I already want?” Generative conversation asks, “What becomes possible when these two minds meet?”

## Message forms that can create anticipation

These are forms, not scripts.

### The return

The sender comes back to something from an earlier exchange after having actually thought about it.

> You said you disliked that book because it made forgiveness sound morally mandatory. I thought you meant the prose at first, but I think you meant the whole bargain. Is that closer?

The important feature is not the question mark. It is evidence that the earlier statement remained active in the sender's mind.

### The offering

The sender brings an object, scene, passage, song, or idea and explains why it belongs in this conversation.

> I heard a song today that does the thing you said you wanted from a love story: it never claims that understanding erases the damage.

“Thought of you” becomes meaningful only when the association is specific enough to reveal both people.

### The answer-first question

The sender does not place the whole burden of disclosure on the recipient.

> I have a question that could sound like an interview, so I will answer it first: the thing I miss most from a place I hated is the walk home after midnight. What have you missed from somewhere you were glad to leave?

### The unfinished judgment

The sender offers a view that is real but revisable.

> I think the character is cruel, but I cannot decide whether the cruelty is the point or whether the author simply failed to see it.

This gives the other person something to alter, not merely affirm.

### The small future

The message places a low-pressure possibility in shared time.

> Keep the photograph. I want to see whether it looks different after you tell me why that room mattered.

This need not be a date invitation. It can be an agreement to return to a work, place, problem, or running joke.

## How Shallow could help without writing the relationship for them

Shallow should not generate polished messages for users to pass off as their own voice. That produces counterfeit attention and makes the other person respond to the model.

It can instead maintain a private **thread memory** for each user:

- subjects the other person introduced;
- unresolved questions;
- recommendations exchanged;
- predictions and disagreements;
- promised follow-ups;
- objects or places that acquired shared meaning;
- things the user said they wanted to think about before answering.

At composition time, Shallow could show the user a small set of reminders, not finished sentences:

- “You said you would listen to the live version.”
- “She distinguished being lonely from wanting a relationship.”
- “He asked what you meant by loyalty after desire ends.”
- “You disagreed about whether the protagonist actually chose.”

The user supplies the judgment and the language.

## Questions generated from evidence, not profile slogans

A strong question should usually satisfy most of these tests:

- It has an identifiable reason for being asked now.
- It grows from something the other person actually did, chose, kept, watched, read, visited, or said.
- The answer cannot be replaced by a demographic field or personality label.
- The asker could answer it too.
- It creates more than one possible next turn.
- It does not demand trauma, confession, or instant emotional nakedness.

Examples from Shallow evidence:

- From a bookshelf photograph: “You have three books from the same period but they do not seem to agree with each other. Did you read them as an argument, or did they arrive at different times in your life?”
- From listening history: “That album disappears for six years and then comes back hard. Do you remember what brought it back?”
- From maps or activity history: “You keep returning to this route but almost never at the same time of day. Is the destination the point, or the movement?”
- From a film the person marked as enduring: “What survived after the plot faded—the scene, the feeling, or the way you first saw it?”

These questions get outside self-description while still allowing the person to interpret their own evidence.

## Failure modes

### The interview

A sequence of unrelated questions communicates evaluation, not interest. Full-switch questions repeatedly abandon what the person just said.

### Forced depth

“Tell me your deepest fear” can produce disclosure without understanding. Depth should be earned by the conversation's history, not scheduled by the app.

### Therapeutic imitation

Constant validation language can feel false and can suppress disagreement, humor, erotic charge, and intellectual friction. People want to be understood, not clinically processed.

### Manufactured scarcity

Delaying delivery, hiding replies, timing notifications, or recommending “wait two days” converts uncertainty into a retention device. Genuine anticipation already contains uncertainty; the app should not counterfeit it.

### Availability discipline

Read receipts, streaks, response-time scores, and “communication compatibility” metrics turn pleasure into duty. They also confuse fast response with care. Research on sub-second timing in spoken conversation does not justify grading asynchronous text replies.

### Premature totalization

The app should not announce “who this person is” after a few exchanges. Excitement partly depends on continued discovery and the possibility of revision.

### Infinite messaging

Text can support intimacy but also sustain projection. A promising exchange should normally acquire a route toward voice, shared activity, or meeting. Anticipated future interaction itself appears to change how people disclose online; endless pre-meeting conversation can build an imagined person that the meeting cannot bear.

## What to measure

Do not optimize primarily for messages sent, reply speed, session length, or notification opens. Those measures reward pressure and compulsion.

Better research questions:

- Did the recipient want *this person's* next message?
- Did the message make the recipient feel accurately noticed?
- Did either person refer back to the exchange later without prompting?
- Did the conversation generate a shared object, distinction, joke, plan, or question?
- Could each person disagree without the thread collapsing?
- Did the exchange make an actual meeting or shared activity more desirable and more informed?
- After meeting, did the text-created impression become more accurate, or did it dissolve?

## Immediate design proposition

The first Shallow messaging feature should not be an AI reply writer. It should be a **private continuation notebook** that helps a user remember what became alive between them and another person.

The unit is not “a fact about the match.” The unit is a thread:

```text
origin -> what each person contributed -> unresolved edge -> possible return
```

That supports the thing the Dostoevsky scene names: not a questionnaire, not a marketable profile, but the hope of finding one person before whom thought does not have to be continually abbreviated.
