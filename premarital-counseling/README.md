# Premarital counseling and marriage preparation

This folder treats premarital counseling as preparation for a legal, economic, sexual, familial, and moral relationship—not merely a communication-skills course.

## Starting problem

People often say they want “commitment” when they may mean one or more of these:

- companionship;
- reliable access to affection, sex, or shared leisure;
- a household partner;
- social recognition;
- financial cooperation;
- children and co-parenting;
- relief from loneliness;
- exclusivity while the relationship remains satisfying;
- a promise that is meant to bind even during periods when one or both people no longer feel like keeping it.

None of the first eight is contemptible. But they are not interchangeable, and only the last begins to state what makes a promise a commitment rather than a present preference.

A useful working definition:

> **Commitment is an attempt to bind future conduct across changes in desire, convenience, and circumstance.**

That definition is incomplete until the couple states:

1. what conduct is promised;
2. for how long;
3. what costs each person is accepting;
4. what conditions justify revision or exit;
5. who or what helps interpret the promise when the parties disagree;
6. what duties survive separation.

A promise with no expected cost may be sincere, but it has not yet been tested as a commitment. A promise with no legitimate exit conditions can become an instrument of coercion. Marriage preparation has to hold both truths at once.

## Six different things commonly called commitment

### Affective attachment

“I love you and want to be with you.” This matters, but it describes a present state. Feelings can support a commitment; they cannot perform all of its work.

### Deliberate dedication

“I intend to organize my choices around this relationship.” This begins to govern alternatives: jobs, housing, time, sex, money, kin, and care.

### Promise

“I give you a claim on my future conduct.” A promise is interpersonal. Breaking it is not merely changing a plan; it wrongs the person to whom it was made unless the promise itself permits the change.

### Institution

“We place the promise inside law, kinship, religion, property rules, public status, or community expectations.” Institutions can make promises legible and enforceable. They can also preserve domination or make escape unjustly difficult.

### Interdependence

“Our lives now contain joint property, debts, children, caregiving, lost earning capacity, immigration status, health insurance, or other dependencies.” These facts create obligations whether or not the relationship still feels romantic.

### Constraint

“I cannot leave because I lack money, safety, legal status, housing, or social permission.” Constraint is not evidence of devotion. A marriage can contain both commitment and constraint, but premarital counseling should never confuse them.

## The questions ordinary compatibility exercises avoid

### Why marriage?

- What do you want marriage to add that an exclusive long-term relationship does not already provide?
- Which parts are legal, economic, religious, familial, sexual, public, or symbolic?
- Do you want to be married, or do you mainly want not to be alone?
- Would you still want this marriage if no wedding, title, social approval, or tax effect followed?
- Would you still want it if companionship became difficult for a year?

### What exactly is promised?

- Is the promise lifelong intention, lifelong duty, or a high threshold before leaving?
- Are fidelity, co-residence, sexual availability, emotional disclosure, financial pooling, caregiving, and parenthood each part of the promise? Which are not?
- Does “for better or worse” include unemployment, depression, disability, infertility, addiction, political or religious change, long absences, sexual incompatibility, and resentment?
- Which sacrifices may one spouse reasonably ask of the other, and which remain gifts rather than duties?
- What does each person believe the other will owe when love is weak but no clear wrongdoing has occurred?

### What ends or suspends the promise?

Possible answers cannot be reduced to “never” versus “whenever I am unhappy.” Couples need to discuss at least:

- physical or sexual violence;
- coercive control, stalking, confinement, or financial captivity;
- threats against children or dependents;
- sustained deception;
- infidelity and the couple's actual definition of it;
- abandonment;
- untreated addiction and repeated dangerous conduct;
- refusal of all repair or negotiation;
- profound changes in religion, politics, sexuality, or desired family form;
- ordinary loss of love without abuse or betrayal.

A couple may disagree about which of these permits separation, requires separation, permits divorce, or leaves continuing duties. The disagreement itself is important information.

### Who interprets the promise?

- Do the two spouses alone decide what the vows mean?
- Do law, clergy, kin, friends, therapists, mediators, elders, or a written agreement have authority?
- What happens when each person sincerely believes the other broke the promise first?
- Is there a required process before separation—conversation, temporary distance, mediation, treatment, financial disclosure, or consultation?
- When is outside involvement helpful, and when does it become social pressure to remain in danger?

### What survives exit?

- Duties to children are the clearest case, but what about a spouse who lost years of earnings while doing care work?
- What happens to debts, housing, pets, family ties, medical information, reproductive material, shared creative work, and private disclosures?
- Is confidentiality still owed?
- Does one spouse owe transitional support even if the law does not compel it?
- What kind of contact, if any, is owed to former stepchildren or in-laws?

## Conversation sequence

The order matters. Couples should not begin with communication technique and end before reaching the material terms of the relationship.

### 1. The proposed marriage

Each person separately writes what they believe they are entering, why marriage rather than continued dating is wanted, and what they believe will become obligatory after the wedding.

### 2. Existing evidence

Replace idealized self-description with examples:

- a decision the couple made when desires conflicted;
- a period when one person carried more of the practical load;
- a disagreement involving money, sex, kin, work, or children;
- a promise each person kept despite inconvenience;
- a promise each person broke and how they handled it;
- an instance when one person felt unsafe, trapped, ignored, or taken for granted.

### 3. Money, property, and labor

At minimum disclose and discuss:

- income and expected volatility;
- all debts, credit reports, tax obligations, liens, support duties, and financial dependents;
- current property and expected inheritances;
- ownership versus use of housing and vehicles;
- individual, joint, and emergency accounts;
- spending authority and thresholds for consultation;
- retirement, insurance, beneficiaries, wills, and incapacity documents;
- unpaid housework, childcare, eldercare, and career sacrifice;
- compensation or protection for a spouse whose earning capacity is reduced by joint decisions;
- prenuptial or postnuptial agreements;
- what each person thinks “fair” means if the marriage ends after two, ten, or thirty years.

Financial disclosure is not a secondary “money personality” exercise. Marriage changes legal and practical exposure to property, debt, labor, dependency, and risk.

### 4. Sex, fidelity, and privacy

Discuss acts and boundaries rather than asking whether each person is simply “traditional” or “open-minded.” Include:

- what counts as sex, infidelity, pornography, flirting, secrecy, and emotional exclusivity;
- desire discrepancy and long periods without sex;
- contraception, abortion, infertility, pregnancy, and sexually transmitted infection testing;
- privacy of devices, journals, messages, location, health information, and friendships;
- whether either person expects sexual conduct to be a marital duty;
- how consent operates inside an ongoing relationship;
- what may be shared with friends, relatives, clergy, or counselors.

### 5. Children and other dependents

- Whether to have children, how many, and under what conditions.
- What happens if fertility, pregnancy, disability, or a child's needs alter the plan.
- Discipline, schooling, religion, language, medical decisions, screens, work, and care.
- Adoption, fostering, stepchildren, donor conception, and reproductive material.
- Responsibilities to parents, siblings, disabled relatives, former partners, and existing children.
- Which adult reduces paid work and how that loss is recognized.

### 6. Work, place, and time

- Whose work determines where the household lives?
- What kinds of moves or schedules require mutual consent?
- How much solitude, friendship, travel, political work, religious practice, or creative work remains individually controlled?
- What happens if one person wants a less lucrative life?
- What does each person regard as abandonment through work, gaming, phone use, family involvement, or hobbies?

### 7. Kin, religion, and community

Marriage often joins networks, not only individuals. Discuss holidays, rituals, burial, conversion, dietary rules, family access, financial support, caregiving, secrets, loyalty conflicts, and what happens when a family member mistreats a spouse.

### 8. Illness, disability, aging, and death

- What care is promised, and what care exceeds either person's capacity?
- How will paid care be financed?
- Who has authority during incapacity?
- What happens to sex, work, housing, and social life during chronic illness?
- What are each person's views on life support, assisted death where legal, organ donation, burial, and inheritance?
- Does remarriage after widowhood conflict with either person's idea of fidelity?

### 9. Conflict and repair

Only here should communication instruction become central. The useful questions are concrete:

- Can either person stop a dangerous or degrading exchange without being punished for leaving the room?
- Does a pause include a definite return?
- Can each person state the other's position accurately before rebutting it?
- What requires apology, restitution, changed behavior, or outside help?
- Which conflicts are decisions, which are recurring differences to manage, and which reveal an incompatible marriage proposal?

### 10. Legal and institutional reality

Read the law of the actual jurisdiction. Discuss marriage licenses, property regime, debt, parentage, adoption, divorce, support, custody, inheritance, benefits, taxes, immigration, medical authority, and domestic-violence protections. Generic counseling material cannot substitute for current legal advice.

## A Shallow format

Shallow should not output a compatibility percentage. A score conceals which assumptions produced it and encourages couples to treat structural disagreements as psychometric distance.

A better format:

1. Each person answers privately in ordinary language.
2. The system identifies where the answers concern the same obligation but use different terms.
3. It displays the two answers side by side without declaring a winner.
4. It asks for concrete past examples and a future scenario.
5. Each person states what they think the other meant.
6. The system records agreements, unresolved differences, and matters requiring legal, financial, medical, religious, or clinical expertise.
7. The couple writes a plain-language commitment statement and a plain-language exit-and-surviving-duties statement.

No hidden weighting. No personality type. No claim that agreement on every item predicts success.

## Evidence standard

Marriage-education research does not justify strong promises.

- Older meta-analyses often found small-to-moderate short-term changes in reported relationship quality or communication, but samples were frequently White, middle-class, self-selected, and followed only for months.
- A large randomized federal evaluation, Building Strong Families, enrolled more than 5,000 unmarried expectant or new-parent couples. Pooled results at three years found no improvement in relationship quality, staying together, or marriage, with substantial variation among sites.
- A 2022 meta-analysis of 32 controlled evaluations of federally funded programs reported small effects on relationship quality and skills and no significant pooled effect on relationship stability.
- An eight-year randomized premarital-intervention study found no overall difference in divorce among PREP, clergy-delivered usual services, and university delivery; effects varied by prior interaction patterns, and some higher-negativity couples assigned to PREP divorced more often.

Therefore:

- treat curricula as organized question inventories, not proven divorce prevention;
- prefer randomized and long-term evidence over participant satisfaction and retrospective self-ratings;
- report null, subgroup, and adverse findings;
- do not repeat commercial claims such as “31% less likely to divorce” without tracing the exact study and population;
- distinguish learning a communication exercise from making a viable marriage;
- never count divorce itself as a negative outcome when separation may end violence, coercion, or an untenable union.
