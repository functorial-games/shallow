# Evidence ledger: premarital and relationship education

The question is not whether participants enjoy a course or can repeat its vocabulary. The relevant outcomes include durable behavior, safety, material cooperation, relationship quality, separation, divorce, co-parenting, child welfare, and whether ending an unsafe relationship becomes easier rather than harder.

## Reading rules

For each study record:

- intervention and comparator;
- who enrolled and who did not;
- random assignment or other design;
- attrition and missing data;
- length of follow-up;
- self-report versus observed or administrative outcome;
- whether the program's developers evaluated their own program;
- preregistration and multiplicity handling where available;
- pooled result and site or subgroup variation;
- null and adverse outcomes;
- whether divorce is automatically coded as failure;
- population limits.

Do not convert a statistically significant small mean effect into a promise that a particular couple will stay married.

## Major reviews and trials

### Carroll and Doherty (2003), premarital-program meta-analysis

- Citation: Jason S. Carroll and William J. Doherty, “Evaluating the Effectiveness of Premarital Prevention Programs: A Meta-Analytic Review of Outcome Research,” *Family Relations* 52(2):105–118. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2003.00105.x.
- Reported result: a large mean standardized effect in the assembled studies, concentrated in immediate and short-term interpersonal-skill and relationship-quality outcomes.
- Authors' important limitation: long-term follow-up was too sparse to support conclusions about durable effects.
- Shallow use: historical evidence of how optimistic early syntheses became; not a basis for a divorce-prevention claim.

### Hawkins et al. (2008), broad marriage-education meta-analysis

- Citation: Alan J. Hawkins, Victoria L. Blanchard, Scott A. Baldwin, and Elizabeth B. Fawcett, “Does Marriage and Relationship Education Work? A Meta-Analytic Study,” *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 76(5):723–734. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012584.
- Scope: 117 studies from 86 codable reports and more than 500 effect sizes.
- Reported controlled-study effects: roughly `d = .30–.36` for relationship quality and `d = .43–.45` for communication skills.
- Limits stated in the paper: little racial, ethnic, or economic diversity; most follow-ups at three to six months; only a handful beyond twelve months; relationship stability and aggression rarely measured; published communication studies had larger follow-up effects than unpublished studies.
- Shallow use: evidence that short-term skill and satisfaction measures can move; also evidence that the field's common outcomes are narrower than the institution of marriage.
- Open record: https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4225/.

### Building Strong Families, federal multisite randomized evaluation

- Intervention: voluntary relationship-skills education and related supports for unmarried couples expecting or recently having a child.
- Design: more than 5,000 couples enrolled and were randomly assigned across eight local programs; the three-year analysis used follow-up data from more than 4,000 couples.
- Three-year pooled result: no improvement in relationship quality, remaining romantically involved, marriage, or co-parenting; small negative effects appeared on some father-involvement outcomes. Site results varied, and patterns seen at fifteen months did not reliably persist.
- Important detail: low attendance did not explain away the pooled null result; analyses among regular attendees produced little evidence of relationship effects.
- Interpretation: a communication-and-relationship curriculum does not automatically overcome economic strain, unstable work, housing, prior trauma, new-parent demands, or local implementation differences.
- Report page: https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/long-term-effects-building-strong-families.
- Executive summary: https://acf.gov/sites/default/files/documents/opre/bsf_36_mo_impact_exec_summ.pdf.
- Journal article: Robert G. Wood et al., “The Effects of Building Strong Families,” *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management* 31(2):228–252. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.21608.

### Supporting Healthy Marriage / Within Our Reach

- Citation: Galena K. Rhoades, “The Effectiveness of the Within Our Reach Relationship Education Program for Couples: Findings from a Federal Randomized Trial,” *Family Process* 54(4):672–685. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/famp.12148.
- Design: secondary analysis of 3,609 low-income married couples randomly assigned in the Supporting Healthy Marriage evaluation.
- Reported result: small average effects on several self-reported couple and individual outcomes at twelve and thirty months, around `d = .14–.15`; no significant effect on the percentage married, cooperative parenting, or severe psychological assault.
- Interpretation: some aspects of reported relationship experience may improve slightly without changing marital stability.
- Shallow use: separate “better average score on selected scales” from “marriage preserved,” and inspect the full outcome family rather than highlighting only significant measures.
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25787758/.

### PREP for Strong Bonds, U.S. Army trial

- Citation: Scott M. Stanley et al., “A Randomized Controlled Trial of Relationship Education in the U.S. Army: 2-Year Outcomes,” *Family Relations* 63(4):484–495. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/fare.12083.
- Design: 662 married Army couples at two installations.
- Result: no overall enduring effect on relationship quality at two years. At the higher-risk site, divorce was lower in the intervention group (`8.1%` versus `14.9%`); the effect did not generalize uniformly across sites and was moderated by participant characteristics.
- Shallow use: preserve site heterogeneity. “The program reduced divorce” is too broad a summary.
- PubMed Central record: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4237282/.

### Eight-year randomized premarital intervention trial

- Citation: Howard J. Markman, Galena K. Rhoades, Scott M. Stanley, and Kristina M. Peterson, “A Randomized Clinical Trial of the Effectiveness of Premarital Intervention: Moderators of Divorce Outcomes,” *Journal of Family Psychology* 27(1):165–172. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031134.
- Design: religious organizations were randomized to PREP or naturally occurring premarital services; professional university delivery was also compared. Group sizes reported in the abstract were 44, 66, and 83 couples.
- Result: no overall difference in divorce over the first eight years of marriage.
- Moderator result: couples showing more negative premarital interaction were more likely to divorce when assigned to PREP than when receiving naturally occurring services, while lower-negativity couples showed the opposite pattern. A similar pattern for prior physical aggression was only marginally significant.
- Interpretation: an average intervention can interact with risk in ways that make “more education” an unsafe default assumption. Divorce can also be a beneficial outcome.
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23421844/.

### Hawkins et al. (2022), federally funded program meta-analysis

- Citation: Alan J. Hawkins and colleagues, “How Effective Are ACF-Funded Couple Relationship Education Programs? A Meta-Analytic Study,” *Family Process* 61(3):970–985. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/famp.12739.
- Scope: 32 controlled studies of adult couple relationship education funded through the U.S. Administration for Children and Families.
- Pooled effects reported in the abstract: relationship quality `d = .114`; relationship skills `d = .132`; mental health `d = .074`; co-parenting `d = .033`.
- Null pooled outcomes: relationship stability, parenting, and child well-being.
- One-group pre/post studies produced larger estimates than controlled studies, reinforcing the danger of treating pre/post change as causal.
- Shallow use: assume effects are small and outcome-specific unless stronger evidence says otherwise.
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35040124/.

## What the evidence does support

At most, the current ledger supports these restrained claims:

1. Some structured programs can produce small average short-term improvements in selected communication, skill, or relationship-quality measures.
2. Results depend on population, site, implementation, dosage, baseline risk, and what outcome is measured.
3. Skill changes do not reliably imply greater relationship stability.
4. Large randomized evaluations have produced important null results and some adverse subgroup or site findings.
5. Economic and institutional problems cannot be safely reframed as deficits in couple communication.
6. A course can help a couple identify a reason not to marry. That is a successful preparation outcome even when it lowers the marriage rate.

## What the evidence does not support

- A universal percentage reduction in divorce for premarital counseling.
- A claim that communication style is the main causal determinant of marital survival.
- A compatibility score that predicts whether two particular people should marry.
- Treating continued marriage as good regardless of violence, coercion, or severe deprivation.
- Assuming results from White, middle-class, heterosexual, voluntarily enrolled couples transfer to low-income parents, arranged marriages, later-life remarriages, LGBTQ couples, disabled couples, or couples embedded in different legal and kinship systems.
- Treating a retrospective report that “I improved” as equivalent to observed change or a controlled comparison.

## Research gaps Shallow should care about

- Whether preparation improves financial disclosure and reduces undisclosed debt.
- Whether couples write more explicit and equitable agreements about unpaid care work.
- Whether participants recognize coercive control earlier.
- Whether preparation changes prenuptial, medical-authority, insurance, beneficiary, will, or parenting decisions.
- Whether people who decide not to marry report better later outcomes than comparable people pushed toward marriage.
- Whether question sets work differently when answered privately before joint discussion.
- Whether facilitators can distinguish ordinary conflict from abuse without placing a victim at greater risk.
- Whether commitment statements predict conduct once desire, health, work, or money changes.
- Whether a legal-and-economic curriculum outperforms communication-centered preparation on outcomes that matter materially.
