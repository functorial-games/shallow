# Feminists

A Shallow reading/research lane for women writing about sex, desire, beauty, femininity, marriage, sexual difference, and the gap between ideology and lived experience.

The point is not to force these writers into one political tendency. Keep disagreements. Prefer first-person evidence, primary texts, interviews, and concrete descriptions of what women say they want or experience.

## Nina Power

Philosopher and writer.

Start with:
- *One-Dimensional Woman*
- *What Do Men Want? Masculinity and Its Discontents*
- Alex Kaschuta / *Subversive*: “Nina Power — Men & Women at the End of History” (2021)
- Alex Kaschuta / *Subversive*: “Nina Power — Loyalty, Friendship, Truth” (2024)

Shallow questions:
- femininity without reducing women to consumer identities
- sex-negativity and the sexual revolution
- marriage and living with sexual difference
- aging, beauty, loyalty, friendship
- men and women as relational rather than isolated market actors

## Cartoons Hate Her

Pseudonymous essayist.

Start with:
- “My Permission to Be Horny” (2025)

Useful because she writes directly about straight female fantasy from inside it rather than treating women’s desire as an abstract variable. Her argument in “My Permission to Be Horny” is that fantasies involving dangerous, dominant, taboo, or otherwise transgressive men may share a deeper function: giving the woman permission to experience and act on desire.

Keep this as first-person/cultural analysis, not universal female psychology.

## Alex Kaschuta

Writer and host of *Subversive*.

Relevant interviews include Nina Power and Cartoons Hate Her.

Shallow questions:
- femininity, motherhood, sexual difference
- marriage and family
- female desire outside the standard liberal/therapeutic vocabulary
- tensions between modern sexual freedom and long-term attachment

## Emily Ratajkowski

Start with:
- *My Body* (2021)

Useful for the ambivalence of beauty. She describes attractiveness and sexual display as producing real money, status, attention, autonomy, and pleasure while also making that power conditional on being desired and making it easy for other people to treat her body as an asset they are entitled to use.

This is a good primary-text counterexample to either simple story:
1. beauty and the gaze are only oppressive;
2. beauty gives women uncomplicated power.

## Shere Hite

Start with:
- *The Hite Report: A Nationwide Study of Female Sexuality* (1976)

Important primary-source material on women describing their own sexual experiences, pleasure, masturbation, orgasm, dissatisfaction, and relationships in enormous numbers.

Treat the sampling and survey design critically, but preserve the raw historical value of women answering questions in their own words.

## Elaine Scarry

Start with:
- *On Beauty and Being Just* (1999)

Not primarily a book about sexual desire. Relevant because Scarry refuses the simple idea that beauty is merely domination by the beholder. Beauty gives pleasure, commands attention, and acts on the person who beholds it.

Useful for thinking about the power and vulnerability involved in being beautiful and being admired.

## Historical female desire

Keep a separate thread on the historical reversal in stereotypes of female sexuality:

- medieval and early-modern traditions often represented women as unusually lustful, bodily, or difficult to sexually govern;
- nineteenth-century Anglo-American “passionlessness” increasingly represented respectable women as naturally less sexual than men;
- modern stereotypes often inherit the second model and treat male desire as the default high-libido case.

Useful starting points:
- Nancy F. Cott, “Passionlessness: An Interpretation of Victorian Sexual Ideology, 1790–1850”
- Kate Lister, *Flick: A History of Female Pleasure*
- *Montaillou* as evidence about actual courtship, affairs, desire, gossip, and attachment rather than prescriptive theory.

The Shallow question is not “which stereotype is correct?” It is: how much of what a period treats as obvious about male and female desire is a period-specific script, and what do people’s actual words and behavior show instead?
