# History of love

Working notes on how love, desire, courtship, jealousy, attachment, separation, and erotic longing have been described across times and cultures.

The point is not to assume that every source is describing one timeless thing called "love." Keep the primary evidence close, distinguish description from theory, and note where later interpreters impose categories on older material.

## Sappho

Sappho is an early anchor because the surviving poems give unusually direct descriptions of erotic attention and bodily disturbance rather than a later theory of love.

Start with:

- **Fragment 31** — desire/jealousy as a bodily event: speech fails, heat under the skin, ringing ears, sweat, trembling.
- **Fragment 16** — beauty reorganized around the particular person one loves; Anactoria appears through absence and memory.
- **Fragment 1 (Hymn to Aphrodite)** — recurring desire, pursuit, refusal, and the wish that the beloved return desire.
- **Fragment 47** — Eros strikes the body like wind falling on mountain oaks.
- **Fragment 94** — separation, memory, and remembered intimacy.
- **Fragment 105a** — the unreachable apple: desirability and unattainability.
- **Fragment 130** — Eros as *glukupikron*, "sweet-bitter": pleasure and pain in one word.

Keep Greek text beside translations when possible.

## Helen Fisher

Helen Fisher (biological anthropologist) spent much of her career studying romantic love, mate choice, attachment, and rejection. Use her work in three separate lanes rather than treating it as one evidentiary package.

### 1. Historical and ethnographic leads

Fisher's talks and books are useful for finding cross-cultural descriptions of love that can then be traced back to their original sources.

In her 2008 TED talk **The Brain in Love**, she reads a love poem she attributes to an anonymous **Kwakiutl** speaker, recorded by a missionary in 1896:

> "Fire runs through my body with the pain of loving you."

The full passage describes fire, bodily pain, remembered words, impending separation, numbness with grief, and farewell. This belongs here as a primary-source lead on the bodily language of passionate attachment and separation.

**Provenance warning:** Fisher calls the speaker "Kwakiutl" and says "southern Alaska." Do not silently relabel this as Inuit. Track down the 1896 source and original ethnographic/linguistic context before treating Fisher's attribution as settled.

### 2. Empirical neuroscience

Keep the actual papers, especially:

- Fisher, H.; Aron, A.; Brown, L. L. (2005). **"Romantic love: An fMRI study of a neural mechanism for mate choice."** *Journal of Comparative Neurology* 493:58–62. DOI: 10.1002/cne.20772.
- Aron, A.; Fisher, H.; Mashek, D. J.; Strong, G.; Li, H.; Brown, L. L. (2005). **"Reward, motivation, and emotion systems associated with early-stage intense romantic love."** *Journal of Neurophysiology* 94:327–337.
- Fisher, H.; Aron, A.; Brown, L. L. (2006). **"Romantic love: A mammalian brain system for mate choice."** *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 361:2173–2186.
- Work on romantic rejection and the persistence of reward/motivation responses after being dumped.

Read these as small neuroimaging studies with specific experimental tasks and samples, not as proof of a complete biological theory of love.

### 3. Fisher's larger theory

Fisher commonly separates:
- **lust**
- **romantic attraction**
- **attachment**

and argues that romantic love is better understood as a motivation/reward system involved in mate choice than as merely an emotion.

This is useful as a hypothesis-generator and vocabulary source. Do not treat the three-system scheme, claims of universality, or broad evolutionary explanations as established merely because the neuroimaging papers show activity in reward-related regions.

### Books / talks to mine

- *Anatomy of Love* (1992; later revised edition)
- *Why We Love: The Nature and Chemistry of Romantic Love* (2004)
- *Why Him? Why Her?* (2009) — include, but treat the personality/neurochemical typology especially skeptically.
- TED 2006: **Why We Love, Why We Cheat**
- TED 2008: **The Brain in Love**
- TED 2016: **Technology Hasn't Changed Love. Here's Why**

### Why she is useful here

Even where Fisher's explanatory framework is too tidy, she collected an enormous number of examples, references, questions, and cross-cultural claims about courtship and attachment. Use her as a map to sources. Then go back to the poems, ethnographies, historical records, experiments, and datasets themselves.
